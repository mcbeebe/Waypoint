<PlainBox>
  You do not need a doctor's note, a diagnosis, or the school's permission to ask for a special
  education evaluation. You need one written request. Once the district receives it, it has
  15 calendar days to send you an assessment plan, and 60 days from the day you sign that plan
  to test your child and hold the IEP meeting. Email the request to the principal and the
  district's special education director, and keep a dated copy.
</PlainBox>

<div class="cols">
<article class="prose">

Take a breath. You are not behind, and you are not overstepping. Asking the school to look
closely at your child is the single most ordinary thing a parent can do in this system — it is
the step every IEP in California begins with, and the law is written to make it easy.

This page is the process: who can ask, what the district owes you and when, how to read the
assessment plan before you sign it, and what to do if the district goes quiet. **The letter
itself is already written for you** at
<GatedLink href="/letters/iep-evaluation-request/">Request an IEP evaluation</GatedLink> — copy
it, fill in the blanks, send it, then come back here for what happens next.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="who-can-ask">Who can ask for an evaluation — and do I need a diagnosis?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  <b>You can</b>, and you need nothing to do it. Federal law says plainly that either a parent or
  the school district may start a request for an initial evaluation
  <Cite>34 CFR §300.301</Cite>. No diagnosis, no doctor's referral, no teacher's blessing. A
  suspicion that your child may have a disability affecting their education is enough — and the
  district already has a standing legal duty to find and assess children like yours
  <Cite>Ed Code §56301</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

That duty has a name: **child find**. California requires that all children with disabilities
residing in the state be identified, located, and assessed <Cite>Ed Code §56301</Cite>. So when
you send your request, you are not asking a district to do you a favor or to make an exception in
your child's case. You are asking it to do something it is already obligated to do, on a schedule
someone else already set.

A few things parents are told that are not the law:

- **"You need a diagnosis first."** No. The school's own evaluation is what determines
  eligibility, and it works from a two-part test — a disability in one of the categories federal
  law names, *and* a resulting need for special education <Cite>34 CFR §300.8</Cite>. A private
  diagnosis is useful evidence. It is not a prerequisite.
- **"Let's try interventions for a few more months first."** Support strategies and an evaluation
  can run at the same time. A request to evaluate does not have to wait behind a tiering process.
- **"Just come to a meeting and we'll talk about it."** A meeting is fine. It is not a substitute
  for a written request, because only the written request starts a clock.

<NoticeBox>
  Put it in writing, and put a date on it. A phone call, a hallway conversation, or a comment at
  a parent-teacher conference starts nothing. The 15-day clock runs from the district's
  <b>receipt</b> of a written request <Cite>Ed Code §56321</Cite> — so email is your friend: it
  timestamps itself.
</NoticeBox>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="how-to-send">How do I actually send it?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Email the letter to <b>both</b> your school's principal and your district's <b>special
  education director</b>, and keep the sent copy. Ask for the evaluation <b>in all areas of
  suspected disability</b>, and name the specific things worrying you. That phrase is what sets
  the scope of everything the district is about to do <Cite>Ed Code §56320</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

The letter does this work for you — it is deliberately warm, short, and collaborative, because the
first ask should always be an ask. Get it at
<GatedLink href="/letters/iep-evaluation-request/">Request an IEP evaluation</GatedLink>.

Three practical notes the letter cannot carry for you:

1. **Send it to two people.** A request that lands only in a teacher's inbox can sit there.
   The principal and the special education director are the two addresses that reliably move it.
2. **Write down what you see, not what you think it is.** "He is not reading words his classmates
   read, and he cries before school three days a week" is more useful to an evaluator than a
   diagnosis you are guessing at. Your observations set the scope; their tests fill it in.
3. **Keep everything in one place** from today: the sent email, the assessment plan, every
   report, and every note. You are starting a file you will use for years.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="the-clocks">What happens next, and by when?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  <b>15 calendar days</b> from the district's receipt of your request, an assessment plan must
  reach you <Cite>Ed Code §56043</Cite>. You then get <b>at least 15 days</b> to decide on it
  <Cite>Ed Code §56321</Cite>. From the day the district receives your signed consent, it has
  <b>60 days</b> to complete the assessments, decide eligibility, and hold the IEP meeting
  <Cite>Ed Code §56344</Cite>. School breaks longer than five schooldays pause both clocks.
</AnswerFirst>

  Assessment plan within <b>15 calendar days</b> of your written request · at least <b>15 days</b>
  for you to review and sign · <b>60 days</b> from your signed consent to the evaluation and IEP
  meeting <span class="cite">Ed Code §56321 · §56043 · §56344</span>
</DeadlineCallout>

**The two exceptions worth knowing before you hit send.** If your request arrives 10 days or fewer
before the regular school year ends, the assessment plan is not due until 10 days after the next
school year starts <Cite>Ed Code §56321</Cite>. And if the referral comes 30 days or fewer before
the year ends, the IEP is due within 30 days after the next school year begins
<Cite>Ed Code §56344</Cite>. A request sent the first week of May and one sent the last week of
May can land a whole summer apart — so if you are near the end of the year, send it now rather
than after the break.

**The breaks pause the clock, but only the long ones.** Neither the 15-day nor the 60-day count
includes days between regular school sessions or terms, or school vacations longer than five
schooldays; when the break ends, the clock picks back up on the day regular schooldays reconvene
<Cite>Ed Code §56321</Cite> <Cite>Ed Code §56344</Cite>. Winter break pauses your clock. A
three-day weekend does not.

**The only lawful way to extend the 15 days is your written agreement**
<Cite>Ed Code §56321</Cite>. If someone asks you for more time, that is a request you may grant —
in writing, with a new date attached — not an announcement.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="assessment-plan">The assessment plan arrived. Should I just sign it?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Read the scope first. The plan lists exactly which areas will be assessed, and the law requires
  your child be assessed <b>in all areas related to the suspected disability</b> — including,
  where relevant, health and development, vision, hearing, motor abilities, language function,
  general intelligence, academic performance, communicative status, self-help, social and
  emotional status, and more <Cite>Ed Code §56320</Cite>. If a concern you raised is missing, ask
  in writing for it to be added <b>before</b> you sign.
</AnswerFirst>

This is the highest-leverage five minutes in the whole process, and it is the one most parents
skip. What is not on the assessment plan does not get tested. What does not get tested does not
appear in the eligibility decision or the IEP. A plan that covers academics but not the behavior
you described, or speech but not sensory or motor needs, quietly narrows everything downstream.

So: put your original letter beside the plan and check them line by line. If something is
missing, reply in writing — plainly, and without drama — naming the concern and asking that it be
added to the plan. Then sign the corrected version.

Two more rights that live in the same statute. **No single test can decide it:** no one measure or
assessment may be used as the sole criterion for eligibility or for determining your child's
program <Cite>Ed Code §56320</Cite>. And **testing has to be fair:** assessments must be given in
the language and form most likely to yield accurate information about what your child knows and
can do, must not be racially, culturally, or sexually discriminatory, and must be administered by
trained personnel <Cite>Ed Code §56320</Cite>. If your child's home language is not English, say
so in writing now, not after the scores come back.

Then sign promptly. Nothing is tested until you do — no IEP results from the assessment without
your consent <Cite>Ed Code §56321</Cite> — and the 60-day clock does not begin until the district
has your signature. Every day the plan sits unsigned is a day nobody is evaluating your child.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="during">What actually happens during the assessment?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  School specialists — typically a school psychologist, and depending on the areas assessed a
  speech-language pathologist, occupational therapist, or resource teacher — test and observe your
  child during the school day, review records, and interview the adults who know them. It is free
  to you. You are part of it: your input is evidence, not background.
</AnswerFirst>

Your job during these weeks is smaller than it feels, and it matters more than it looks:

- **Write down the hard days.** A two-week log — what happened, when, how long it took to recover —
  gives an evaluator the pattern you live and they cannot see in a testing room.
- **Share what you already have.** Outside evaluations, therapy reports, doctor's notes,
  old report cards. Send them in and ask that they be considered.
- **Ask for your child's records.** You are entitled to inspect them, and the district owes them
  within **five business days** of your request <Cite>Ed Code §56043</Cite>. Read the file before
  the meeting, not during it.
- **Ask for the reports in advance.** There is no rule that hands them to you before the meeting,
  so ask — politely, in writing, about a week out. Reading 40 pages of scores cold, in a room of
  strangers, is not a fair way to make decisions about your child.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="stall">What if the district stalls, or says no?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Missed deadlines are not something you have to accept quietly. On <b>day 16</b> with no
  assessment plan, follow up in writing and name the date the district received your request
  <Cite>Ed Code §56321</Cite>. If the district <b>refuses</b> to assess, it owes you <b>prior
  written notice</b> explaining why and listing what it relied on <Cite>Ed Code §56500.4</Cite> —
  and a refusal to initiate an assessment is one of the grounds you may take to a due process
  hearing <Cite>Ed Code §56501</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

Work it in this order. Each rung costs more than the last, and most requests never get past the
first two.

1. **Day 16 — a written follow-up.** Short and factual: the date of your original request, the
   fact that no assessment plan has arrived, and the 15-calendar-day rule
   <Cite>Ed Code §56321</Cite>. Copy the special education director. Most silences end here,
   because the deadline is real and staff know it.
2. **Ask for the refusal in writing.** If someone tells you no — in a meeting, on the phone, in a
   hallway — that has no legal weight and starts no clock. Say: *"Please send me prior written
   notice of that decision."* The notice must describe the refusal, explain the reasoning, list
   every record or report relied on, and name the other options the team considered and why they
   were rejected <Cite>Ed Code §56500.4</Cite>. That document is what converts a shrug into
   something reviewable.
3. **Day 61 with no IEP meeting.** Follow up the same way, naming your consent date and the
   60-day rule <Cite>Ed Code §56344</Cite>. Ask for a scheduled date in the reply.
4. **Escalate.** If writing does not move it, the ladder continues: a state compliance complaint
   about a missed timeline, mediation, or a due process filing. Which one fits depends on whether
   your problem is a broken deadline or a disagreement about substance — that choice is the whole
   subject of <GatedLink href="/guides/iep/dispute-ladder/">disagreeing with the school</GatedLink>.

One more card in your hand for later: if the assessments come back and you **disagree with them**,
you may ask for an independent educational assessment at public expense, and the district must
either pay for it or file for a due process hearing to show its own assessment was appropriate
<Cite>Ed Code §56329</Cite>. Keep that one in your pocket until you have read the reports.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="parallel">Start the Regional Center clock at the same time</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  School is one of <b>two</b> free evaluation doors, and they do not talk to each other. If your
  child may have a developmental disability — or is under 3 — request a Regional Center intake and
  assessment now, in parallel. Neither clock waits for the other, and the Regional Center door is
  where respite and Medi-Cal help live.
</AnswerFirst>

Nothing about starting one weakens the other. The school evaluates for educational eligibility;
the Regional Center evaluates for Lanterman Act eligibility, and funds a completely different set
of services. Families who run both from day one are typically a year ahead of families who run
them in sequence. The
<GatedLink href="/guides/regional-centers/">Regional Center guide</GatedLink> has the intake
script and the clocks, and the
<a href="/tools/regional-center-finder/">Regional Center finder</a> tells you which of the 21
centers serves your address.

<EmpathyCallout label="Why this matters">
  Sending this request can feel like an admission — that something is wrong, that you waited too
  long, that you are about to become the difficult parent. It is none of those things. It is one
  email that obliges an entire district to look carefully at your child and tell you, in writing,
  what it sees. Whatever the answer turns out to be, you will know more about your child in
  90 days than you do today. That is what you just set in motion. You did this.
</EmpathyCallout>
