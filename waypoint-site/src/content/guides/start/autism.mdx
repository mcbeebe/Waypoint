<p class="lede" style="margin-top:0">
  Maybe something just feels off, or your child isn't quite where you expected them to be.
  Maybe a caregiver keeps mentioning things, the preschool teacher seems to be trying to tell
  you something gently, or daycare isn't going well and nobody's saying why. Trust that
  signal. The move isn't to wait and see — it's to <b>ask the people who spend their days
  with your child what they're noticing, listen, and get an evaluation</b>. And if the
  diagnosis has already landed: take a breath. Either way, there is a sequence that works —
  your first 100 days, in order, with why each step unlocks the next. Most of it applies
  whatever the answer turns out to be — autism, ADHD, a speech delay, something still
  unnamed. You don't have to do it all today. You just have to start one step.
</p>

<PhaseLabel>Step 0 · Days 0–14 · Get the evaluations moving</PhaseLabel>

<CheckItem id="eval-two-tracks" title="Ask the professionals around your child — then start both evaluation tracks">
  If a teacher, caregiver, or daycare provider has been hinting at something, ask them
  directly what they're seeing — they spend hours a day with your child and compare against
  dozens of others, so their hunch is information, not a verdict. What they tell you points
  you at the evaluations, and there are two separate doors you don't have to pick between: a
  <b> school-district evaluation</b> decides eligibility for school services (free, any
  suspected disability — ADHD included), and a <b>clinical evaluation</b> produces the
  medical diagnosis that insurance-funded therapy requires. They use different criteria, neither substitutes for the
  other, and running both at once is the recognized best move [TBC: verify + cite]. If your
  child is school-age or old enough for preschool (3+), the school track is usually the
  fastest free start — the district must evaluate residents on request even before enrollment
  [TBC: verify Child Find mechanics]. Under 3? Early Start is your first call instead.
</CheckItem>

<CheckItem id="medical-eval-waitlist" title="Get on a clinical evaluation waitlist now — even while the school process runs">
  Start with your pediatrician and ask for a referral for a <b>diagnostic evaluation</b>. For
  autism, that evaluation typically comes from a <b>developmental-behavioral pediatrician,
  child neurologist, or child psychologist</b> — and your pediatrician may be able to make the
  diagnosis themselves, which the American Academy of Pediatrics supports precisely because
  specialist waitlists run long [TBC: verify AAP framing]. The written diagnostic report is
  the key that opens insurance-funded therapy. Waitlists are the bottleneck — get on one this
  week, and take the earliest slot anywhere reasonable.
</CheckItem>

<PhaseLabel>Days 0–30 · Get in every line at once</PhaseLabel>

<CheckItem id="rc-intake-call" title="Call your Regional Center and ask for an intake appointment">
  The evaluation is free, regardless of your income — that's the Lanterman Act
  <span class="cite">WIC §4500</span>. If your child is under 3, Early Start requires an
  eligibility decision within <b>45 days</b>; at 3 and older, within <b>120 days</b> of intake
  <span class="cite">WIC §4643</span>. This one call opens the path to respite and Medi-Cal.
  One honest fit note: Regional Centers serve autism, intellectual disability, cerebral
  palsy, epilepsy, and closely related conditions — <b>ADHD alone usually doesn't qualify</b>
  [TBC: verify], and that's fine: the school and insurance doors below are open to every
  diagnosis. Not sure which of the 21 centers is yours?
  <a class="link" href="/tools/regional-center-finder/">Find your Regional Center →</a>
</CheckItem>

<CheckItem id="school-eval-request" title="Request the school evaluation — in writing, today">
  The moment your district receives a written request, a <b>15-calendar-day</b> clock starts
  for them to respond with an assessment plan <span class="cite">CA Ed Code §56321</span>. A
  phone call starts nothing; a letter starts everything — and this works for any suspected
  disability, ADHD included. Use our ready-to-send version:
  <a class="link" href="/letters/iep-evaluation-request/">IEP evaluation request letter →</a>
</CheckItem>

<CheckItem id="insurance-coverage" title="Ask two questions: what does your child's clinician recommend, and what will insurance cover?">
  Waypoint doesn't push any particular therapy — your child's doctor or evaluating clinician
  is the right source for <b>what</b> to pursue. Your job is the coverage side: call your
  insurer and ask what services and therapies your plan covers for your child's diagnosis,
  and what needs pre-authorization. Useful backstop: for autism, California's <b>SB 946</b>
  requires most fully-insured plans to cover <b>prescribed behavioral health treatment</b>
  [TBC: verify scope] (self-funded ERISA plans are exempt — ask which kind yours is), and
  every plan owes you a straight answer for any diagnosis. Whatever they say no to, get in
  writing: written denials can be appealed, all the way to an Independent Medical Review.
</CheckItem>

<PhaseLabel>Days 31–60 · Build the paper trail — and your people</PhaseLabel>

<CheckItem id="document-binder" title="Start a document binder">
  Every evaluation, letter, Notice of Action, and denial in one place, in date order. Systems
  respond to paper — the family with the organized record is the family that gets the faster
  yes, and the stronger appeal when the answer is no.
</CheckItem>

<CheckItem id="medi-cal-deeming" title="If your child has a developmental disability: apply for Medi-Cal through institutional deeming">
  A child with a developmental disability living at home can qualify for Medi-Cal with
  <b> parental income ignored</b>, via an HCBS waiver. Medi-Cal is the door to IHSS and more —
  your Regional Center service coordinator can help you start the application. (This runs
  through Regional Center eligibility, so it applies to the same diagnoses as that door.)
</CheckItem>

<CheckItem id="find-your-people" title="Find the parents six months ahead of you">
  Every California county has a <b>Family Resource Center</b> and a federally funded
  <b>Parent Training and Information Center</b> staffed largely by parents who've walked this
  road — free, in multiple languages [TBC: verify which orgs serve which counties before
  naming them]. Ask for a parent group for your child's diagnosis; diagnosis-specific
  communities (local autism groups, CHADD for ADHD [TBC: verify]) are where the practical
  knowledge lives. One conversation with a parent six months ahead of you is worth a week of
  searching.
</CheckItem>

<CheckItem id="support-network" title="Build your own support network — on purpose">
  Brief one relative or friend properly — what the diagnosis means, what help looks like — so
  you have one person who gets it. Ask your Regional Center about parent support groups and
  put your first <b>respite request in writing</b> while you're at it. And check on yourself
  the way you'd check on a friend: caregiver burnout is a predictable load, not a personal
  failing, and getting support early is how you stay in this for the long haul.
</CheckItem>

<CheckItem id="higher-support-needs" title="If your child's needs are more significant: start the safety-and-support layer now">
  For children with higher support needs — constant supervision, elopement, self-injury,
  significant medical needs — three moves matter early: describe your <b>hardest days
  honestly</b> in every evaluation (services follow documented need, not best days); ask about
  <b>IHSS protective supervision</b> once Medi-Cal is in place — it can pay a parent for
  supervision hours (<a class="link" href="/guides/benefits/ihss-protective-supervision/">the
  guide →</a>); and start a simple <b>safety log</b> of incidents — it becomes the evidence
  for every hours assessment later. For milder presentations, skip this step without guilt —
  the school and insurance tracks may be your whole first 100 days, and that's a complete
  plan.
</CheckItem>

<PhaseLabel>Days 61–100 · First meetings, prepared</PhaseLabel>

<CheckItem id="ipp-prep" title="Prepare for your first IPP meeting (if Regional Center–eligible)">
  The Individual Program Plan <span class="cite">WIC §4646</span> is where Regional Center
  services actually get written down. Bring your goals in writing and ask for each one by
  name. If something is refused, request a written Notice of Action — that document is what
  carries your appeal rights.
</CheckItem>

<CheckItem id="iep-prep" title="Prepare for the IEP meeting">
  Read the evaluation before the meeting, not at the table. List what you want as measurable
  goals, ask how progress will be reported, and remember: you can take the IEP home to read
  before signing. You never have to decide in the room.
</CheckItem>

<CheckItem id="review-and-rest" title="Breathe, then review">
  Look back over days 0–100: what's working, what's stalled, and which clock needs a written
  nudge. Then rest. The systems will still be there tomorrow — and now you know how to move
  them.
</CheckItem>

<PlainBox tag="Why this order">
  <span id="start-why"></span>
  Evaluations gate everything, so step 0 starts both tracks — educational and clinical — in
  parallel, because they're separate doors that don't wait for each other. Days 0–30 get you
  in every other line at once: every clock — Regional Center, school, insurance — runs
  independently. Days 31–60 build the paper trail those systems will ask for, and the people
  who'll carry you through them. Days 61–100 walk into the first meetings prepared. That is
  the whole ladder — nothing hidden.
</PlainBox>
