<PlainBox>
  IHSS protective supervision can pay a parent up to the program's monthly maximum —
  <b>195 hours a month</b>, or <b>283</b> if your child is severely impaired — to keep a child
  safe who needs constant supervision. You do
  <b> not</b> need to be low-income, and you do <b>not</b> need SSI. The key that opens the
  door is Medi-Cal — and a child with a developmental disability living at home can usually get
  Medi-Cal through <b>institutional deeming</b>, which ignores parental income.
</PlainBox>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="what-is">What is protective supervision?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Protective supervision is the part of In-Home Supportive Services (IHSS) that pays for
  someone — often you, the parent — to <b>observe a child who, because of a developmental
  disability, would put themselves in danger without it</b>. It is about the safety need, not
  chores. The rule lives at <Cite>MPP §30-757.17</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

Think of it as the state recognizing a specific kind of work: the constant, eyes-on vigilance
a child needs when they cannot judge danger for themselves. A child who elopes out the front
door, climbs to unsafe heights without hesitation, mouths inedible objects, or walks toward a
busy street with no sense of risk needs someone watching — not sometimes, but all the time
they are awake.

Two things the county is looking for: your child is **non-self-directing** — meaning that,
compared with a typical child the same age, they cannot be trusted to keep themselves safe
because of impaired judgment, memory, or awareness — and the need for observation is
**constant**, not occasional. A typical five-year-old also needs supervision; protective
supervision covers the supervision your child needs **beyond** what any child that age would
need.

One important boundary: protective supervision is not for housework, meal prep, or medical
procedures — IHSS has other service categories for personal care tasks. Protective supervision
is the category that recognizes round-the-clock watchfulness as a service in itself.

#### What the county is looking for

- **Your child is non-self-directing** — they lack the judgment to keep themselves safe in typical situations, compared with a typical child their age
- **Supervision is constant and beyond typical parenting** — your child needs someone watching closely most waking hours, ready to intervene

<h2 class="qa-h" id="ssi-myth">Does my child need SSI to get IHSS?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  <b>No.</b> IHSS requires <b>Medi-Cal, not SSI</b>. Nothing in <Cite>MPP §30-757.17</Cite>
  requires an SSI award. A child with a developmental disability who lives at home can qualify
  for Medi-Cal through <b>institutional deeming</b> — an HCBS waiver rule that ignores parental
  income entirely.
</AnswerFirst>

This is the single most repeated myth online, and it stops families before they start. Here is
where it comes from: in California, an SSI award brings automatic Medi-Cal with it. So for
many families, SSI happened first and Medi-Cal followed — and the internet quietly rewrote
that sequence as a requirement. It never was one.

If your family income is too high for SSI, you have lost nothing. Ask your
<a href="/guides/regional-centers/">Regional Center</a> service coordinator about institutional
deeming for Medi-Cal, then apply for IHSS.

**What to do:**
- Call your county DHCS office and ask: "Does my child qualify for Medi-Cal under institutional deeming, even if our household income is above the SSI limit?"
- <a href="/tools/ssi-deeming-calculator/">Use our SSI deeming calculator</a> to estimate your situation
- We wrote a short, shareable version of this answer at <a href="/answers/does-my-child-need-ssi-to-get-ihss/">Does my child need SSI to get IHSS?</a>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="apply">How do I apply, step by step?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  You apply through your <b>county IHSS office</b>, then submit two forms: <b>SOC 873</b> (the
  IHSS health care certification every applicant needs, signed by your child's physician) and
  <b>SOC 821</b> (the protective supervision assessment your child's physician completes). A
  county social worker then visits your home to assess need and awards hours — up to the
  monthly maximum of <b>195</b>, or <b>283</b> if your child is severely impaired.
</AnswerFirst>

#### Step 1: Confirm Medi-Cal

**Why this matters:** IHSS requires Medi-Cal. If Medi-Cal is inactive or denied because of household income, IHSS won't happen.

**What to do:**
- Call your county DHCS office and confirm your child's Medi-Cal status is active
- If income was cited as a reason for denial: Ask specifically, "Can you explain institutional deeming and whether it might apply to my child?"
- Write down your child's Medi-Cal number (you'll need it for the IHSS application)

**Timeline:** Same day or next business day

#### Step 2: Apply to Your County IHSS Office

**Why this matters:** The IHSS office needs to know you're specifically applying for *protective supervision*. Using those exact words routes your case to the right team.

**What to do:**
- Find your county IHSS office:
  - Search "[Your County] IHSS office" online
  - Or call 211 for local resources
  - Or visit <a href="https://www.dhcs.ca.gov/services/MediCal/Pages/IHSS-Offices.aspx">DHCS IHSS offices directory</a>
- **Call and say clearly:** "I want to apply for IHSS **protective supervision** for my child" (use those exact words — it routes correctly)
- Alternative: If your county accepts online applications, use their portal
- Ask them to mail or email the application forms to you
- Write down your case number when they give it

**What to expect:** Brief screening questions about your child's needs; confirmation that forms are being sent; a case number for your records

**Timeline:** 5–10 business days to receive forms by mail

#### Step 3: Submit SOC 873 + SOC 821

**Why this matters:** These forms tell the county your child has a medical need for protective supervision and describe exactly what that need is.

**SOC 873 (Health Care Certification):**
- Get a blank form from the county or download from their website
- Schedule an appointment with your child's doctor (pediatrician, developmental pediatrician, or provider who knows your child's disability)
- Bring the form to the appointment and ask the doctor to complete and sign it
- <a href="/resources/sample-doctor-request-for-assessment-of-need-ihss-3130">Use our sample letter</a> if you want to send a formal request first
- Collect the signed form; you'll submit it with SOC 821

**SOC 821 (Protective Supervision Supplemental Form):**
- Get a blank SOC 821 (included in your application packet) or download from county website
- Write down 3–5 specific examples of times your child was about to get hurt and you had to intervene (with dates if you can remember)
- Fill out the form clearly, using those examples
- Describe specific safety risks (climbing, wandering, eating unsafe items, running into traffic, accessing hazardous materials)
- Estimate hours needed per day

**Before you submit — Start your supervision log NOW:**

Even before you submit, begin writing down every day:
- **Time**
- **What your child was doing or trying to do**
- **What danger would have happened if you hadn't intervened**
- **What you did to stop it**

*Example:*
> *9:15am: Found son standing on bathroom sink, reaching for medicine cabinet. Lifted him down, moved ladder out of house.*
> 
> *2:45pm: Daughter tried to open front door to run into the street. She doesn't understand car danger. Redirected her, locked door.*
> 
> *6:30am: Son eating cat food from pantry. Removed him, closed door.*

A supervision log is your strongest evidence. Two weeks of daily entries tells the assessor more than an hour of conversation can.

**What to do:**
- Fill out both forms clearly
- Start your supervision log immediately (keep it running)
- Submit both SOC 873 and SOC 821 together to your county IHSS office (by mail, online portal, or in person)
- Ask for confirmation of receipt or tracking number

**Timeline:** 2–3 weeks to prepare and submit (waiting for doctor signature may take longest)

#### Step 4: Home Assessment Visit

**Why this matters:** This is the county's chance to see firsthand whether your child truly needs constant supervision. Your honest, specific answers matter.

**What to expect:**
- The social worker calls to schedule (usually 30–45 days after you submit forms)
- Visit lasts 30–45 minutes
- They observe your child playing, moving around, interacting
- They ask detailed questions about what a typical day looks like

**What to do before the visit:**
- Write down 4–5 real examples from the past week: what your child was about to do, what you had to do to stop it
- Have your supervision log visible and up to date (they may ask to see it; don't read it aloud, just show you're tracking)
- Clean up obvious safety hazards (unlocked doors, accessible medication, etc.) — but don't hide the normal home
- Make sure your child is well-rested and fed so they're acting like their typical self

**What to do during the visit:**
- Answer questions honestly and directly
- Use specific, concrete language:
  - ✗ "My child needs supervision all the time"
  - ✓ "Last Tuesday, I found them on the roof. Yesterday, they grabbed cleaning supplies. This morning, they were trying to open the front door to run into the street. These things happen almost daily."
- Let the social worker observe your child naturally — don't perform or coach them
- Ask clarifying questions if you don't understand something

**Timeline:** ~30–45 days after you submit forms (timelines vary by county)

#### Step 5: Hours Awarded

**What you'll receive:**
- A letter stating the hours approved (up to 195/month, or 283 if severely impaired)
- The county's reasoning and how they calculated it
- **Your appeal rights** (usually 30 days from the letter date to file an appeal if you disagree)

**What to do:**
- Read the letter carefully and check the math
- Call the county to ask: "How did you arrive at this number of hours?"
- If you agree with the hours: call to set up your provider (you, a family member, or someone else) and payment schedule
- **If you disagree**, see the "Appeal" section below

**Timeline:** Usually 20–30 days after your home visit

<h2 class="qa-h" id="appeal">What if the county low-balls the hours?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  You can push back, and families regularly win on appeal. <b>Ask for the county's assessment
  in writing</b>, then <b>appeal through a state hearing</b>. Your strongest evidence is a
  <b>supervision log</b> documenting your child's actual supervision needs.
</AnswerFirst>

The county's hours decision is not final. If you believe the awarded hours don't match your child's actual supervision needs, you have **30 days to file for a state hearing**.

#### Step 1: Request the County's Written Assessment

**Why this matters:** Their assessment shows you exactly what they think your child needs and why they didn't award more hours. This becomes the foundation of your appeal.

**What to do:**
- Send a letter or email to your county IHSS office:
  > "Please provide the assessment and hour calculation for my child's IHSS protective supervision award dated [date from your decision letter]."
- Keep a copy for yourself
- Ask for confirmation of receipt
- Wait for their response (typically 5–10 business days)

#### Step 2: File for a State Hearing (Within 30 Days)

**Why this matters:** You're formally asking for an independent review of the county's decision by someone outside the county office.

**What to do:**
- Check the date on your award decision letter. You have **30 days from that date** to appeal.
- Call your county IHSS office and say: "I want to request a state hearing to appeal this IHSS decision."
- They'll explain the process and mail you a hearing notice with your hearing date, location/dial-in number, and deadline to submit evidence
- Write down all deadlines and put them in your calendar

**What to expect:**
- Hearing typically happens 30–60 days after you file
- You may appear in person or by phone
- You'll tell your child's story; the county will tell theirs
- An administrative law judge will listen and make a decision
- You may have legal representation, but it's not required

**Timeline:** 30–60 days from filing to hearing

#### Step 3: Build Your Case With Concrete Evidence

**What wins appeals:** Specific, documented evidence of your child's actual supervision needs.

**Your strongest piece of evidence: Your supervision log**

A strong log shows:
- Two weeks (or more) of daily entries
- An entry for every intervention (or most of them)
- Each entry includes: date, time, what happened, what danger you prevented, what you did
- A pattern of constant need for supervision

*Example log entries:*
> *Sept 3, 9:15am: Child found standing on bathroom sink, reaching for medicine cabinet. I lifted them down, moved ladder out of house.*
> 
> *Sept 3, 1:30pm: Child tried to climb out the back gate. I redirected and locked the latch.*
> 
> *Sept 3, 6:00pm: Child grabbed scissors and was heading toward baby sibling. I removed scissors and redirected.*

**Other evidence that strengthens your appeal:**
- A letter from your child's doctor describing their disability and safety needs
- Input from your <a href="/guides/regional-centers/">Regional Center</a> service coordinator (they can write a letter supporting your assessment)
- Photos or videos of safety hazards in your home (if they help show why supervision is needed)
- Documentation of any injuries or near-misses (medical records, incident reports, photos)
- Descriptions of your child's developmental level and how it compares to typical children their age

**What to do before the hearing:**
- Organize your supervision log — make it easy to reference during the hearing
- Write a one-page summary of your child's supervision needs (2–3 key examples)
- Gather supporting documents (doctor's letter, RC coordinator's input, photos)
- Prepare 3–4 specific stories you can tell about a typical day and the supervision your child needs
- Practice explaining these clearly and factually (administrative judges respond to facts, not sentiment)

**At the hearing:**
- Tell your story clearly and specifically
- Use your log and documentation to back it up
- Answer the judge's questions directly
- Don't exaggerate — stick to what you know and have documented
- Bring extra copies of your evidence to give to the judge

**Timeline:** 2–4 weeks after the hearing, you'll receive the judge's decision by mail

<h2 class="qa-h" id="before-you-apply">Before You Apply: Checklist</h2>

Do you have all of these? If not, get them in place first.

- Your child has **active Medi-Cal** — call your county DHCS office to confirm
- You know your **county IHSS office** — find it online or call 211
- You understand what **"protective supervision" means** — it's constant, eyes-on watching to prevent danger (not general childcare or housework)
- You can describe **specific examples** — think of 3–5 times in the past week your child was about to get hurt and you had to intervene
- You have a sense of **how many hours per day** — roughly how many waking hours does your child need supervision?

Not ready yet? Talk to your <a href="/guides/regional-centers/">Regional Center</a> service coordinator — they can help guide you and answer questions before you apply.
