<PlainBox>
  IHSS protective supervision can pay a parent for up to <b>195 hours a month</b> (283 if your
  child is severely impaired) to keep a child safe who needs constant supervision. You do
  <b> not</b> need to be low-income, and you do <b>not</b> need SSI. The key that opens the
  door is Medi-Cal — and a child with a developmental disability living at home can usually get
  Medi-Cal through <b>institutional deeming</b>, which ignores parental income.
</PlainBox>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="what-is">What is protective supervision?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Protective supervision is the part of In-Home Supportive Services (IHSS) that pays for
  someone — often you, the parent — to <b>observe a child who, because of a developmental
  disability, would put themselves in danger without it</b>. It is about the safety need, not
  chores. The rule lives at <Cite>MPP §30-757</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

Think of it as the state recognizing a specific kind of work: the constant, eyes-on vigilance
a child needs when they cannot judge danger for themselves. A child who elopes out the front
door, climbs to unsafe heights without hesitation, mouths inedible objects, or walks toward a
busy street with no sense of risk needs someone watching — not sometimes, but all the time
they are awake.

Two things the county is looking for: your child is **non-self-directing** — meaning that,
compared with a typical child the same age, they cannot be trusted to keep themselves safe
because of impaired judgment, memory, or awareness — and the need for observation is
**constant**, not occasional. A typical five-year-old also needs supervision; protective
supervision covers the supervision your child needs **beyond** what any child that age would
need.

One important boundary: protective supervision is not for housework, meal prep, or medical
procedures — IHSS has other service categories for personal care tasks. Protective supervision
is the category that recognizes round-the-clock watchfulness as a service in itself.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="ssi-myth">Does my child need SSI to get IHSS?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  <b>No.</b> IHSS requires <b>Medi-Cal, not SSI</b>. Nothing in <Cite>MPP §30-757</Cite>
  requires an SSI award. A child with a developmental disability who lives at home can qualify
  for Medi-Cal through <b>institutional deeming</b> — an HCBS waiver rule that ignores parental
  income entirely.
</AnswerFirst>

This is the single most repeated myth online, and it stops families before they start. Here is
where it comes from: in California, an SSI award brings automatic Medi-Cal with it. So for
many families, SSI happened first and Medi-Cal followed — and the internet quietly rewrote
that sequence as a requirement. It never was one.

If your family income is too high for SSI, you have lost nothing. Ask your
<a href="/guides/regional-centers/">Regional Center</a> service coordinator about institutional
deeming for Medi-Cal, then apply for IHSS. We wrote a short, shareable version of this answer
at <a href="/answers/does-my-child-need-ssi-to-get-ihss/">Does my child need SSI to get
IHSS?</a> — and if you are curious whether SSI is on the table anyway, run the
<a href="/tools/ssi-deeming-calculator/">SSI deeming calculator</a>.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="apply">How do I apply, step by step?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  You apply through your <b>county IHSS office</b>, then submit two forms: <b>SOC 821</b> (the
  protective supervision assessment) and <b>SOC 873</b> (a health care certification signed by
  your child's physician). A county social worker then visits your home to assess need and
  awards hours — up to <b>195/month</b>, or <b>283/month</b> if your child is severely
  impaired.
</AnswerFirst>

Before the home visit, start a supervision log — a simple notebook works. Each time you
intervene to keep your child safe, write down the time and what happened. Two weeks of real
entries tells the assessor more than an hour of conversation can.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="appeal">What if the county low-balls the hours?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  You can push back, and families regularly win on appeal. <b>Ask for the county's assessment
  in writing</b>, then <b>appeal through a state hearing</b>. Your strongest evidence is a
  <b>supervision log</b> documenting a 24-hour need.
</AnswerFirst>

First, ask the county — in writing — for the assessment and hour calculations behind the
decision. You are entitled to see how they arrived at the number, and the math often reveals
the gap: an assessor who never saw a meltdown, a form that undercounted nighttime waking, a
"typical day" that was actually your easiest one.

Second, file for a state hearing. The written notice that came with your hours states the
deadline for appealing — calendar it the day the notice arrives. At the hearing you tell your
child's story to someone outside the county office, with your documentation in hand.

Third, build the log. Specifics win hearings. For example: a two-week log with an entry for
every safety intervention — "6:42am, blocked door, attempted to leave house"; "1:15pm, removed
non-food item from mouth"; "3:05am, awake and climbing furniture" [TBC: example log entries —
confirm they read as illustrative, not as claims]. The pattern you live every day becomes
evidence the moment you write it down.
