  IHSS protective supervision can pay a parent up to the program's monthly maximum —
  <b>195 hours a month</b>, or <b>283</b> if your child is severely impaired — to keep a child

<h2 class="qa-h" id="what-is">What is protective supervision?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Protective supervision is the part of In-Home Supportive Services (IHSS) that pays for
  someone — often you, the parent — to <b>observe a child who, because of a developmental
  disability, would put themselves in danger without it</b>. It is about the safety need, not
  chores. The rule lives at <Cite>MPP §30-757.17</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

Think of it as the state recognizing a specific kind of work: the constant, eyes-on vigilance
a child needs when they cannot judge danger for themselves. A child who elopes out the front
door, climbs to unsafe heights without hesitation, mouths inedible objects, or walks toward a
busy street with no sense of risk needs someone watching — not sometimes, but all the time
they are awake.

Two things the county is looking for: your child is **non-self-directing** — meaning that,
compared with a typical child the same age, they cannot be trusted to keep themselves safe
because of impaired judgment, memory, or awareness — and the need for observation is
**constant**, not occasional. A typical five-year-old also needs supervision; protective
supervision covers the supervision your child needs **beyond** what any child that age would
need.

One important boundary: protective supervision is not for housework, meal prep, or medical
procedures — IHSS has other service categories for personal care tasks. Protective supervision
is the category that recognizes round-the-clock watchfulness as a service in itself.

#### What the county is looking for

- **Your child is non-self-directing** — they lack the judgment to keep themselves safe in typical situations, compared with a typical child their age
- **Supervision is constant and beyond typical parenting** — your child needs someone watching closely most waking hours, ready to intervene

<h2 class="qa-h" id="ssi-myth">Does my child need SSI to get IHSS?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  <b>No.</b> IHSS requires <b>Medi-Cal, not SSI</b>. Nothing in <Cite>MPP §30-757.17</Cite>
  requires an SSI award. A child with a developmental disability who lives at home can qualify
  for Medi-Cal through <b>institutional deeming</b> — an HCBS waiver rule that ignores parental
  income entirely.
</AnswerFirst>

This is the single most repeated myth online, and it stops families before they start. Here is
where it comes from: in California, an SSI award brings automatic Medi-Cal with it. So for
many families, SSI happened first and Medi-Cal followed — and the internet quietly rewrote
that sequence as a requirement. It never was one.

If your family income is too high for SSI, you have lost nothing. Ask your
<a href="/guides/regional-centers/">Regional Center</a> service coordinator about institutional
deeming for Medi-Cal, then apply for IHSS.

**What to do:**
1. Call your county DHCS office and ask: "Does my child qualify for Medi-Cal under institutional deeming, even if our household income is above the SSI limit?"
2. <a href="/tools/ssi-deeming-calculator/">Use our SSI deeming calculator</a> to estimate your situation
3. We wrote a short, shareable version of this answer at <a href="/answers/does-my-child-need-ssi-to-get-ihss/">Does my child need SSI to get IHSS?</a>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="apply">How do I apply?</h2>

The application process has five steps — from confirming Medi-Cal through receiving your hours award. It typically takes **8–11 weeks**.

**<a href="/guides/benefits/ihss-protective-supervision-apply/">Read the detailed step-by-step application guide</a>** for concrete actions, timelines, and what to expect at each stage. It includes:

- Confirming your child's Medi-Cal eligibility
- Contacting your county IHSS office
- Submitting SOC 873 and SOC 821 forms (with guidance on the critical supervision log)
- Preparing for your home assessment visit
- Understanding your hours award and appeal rights

The detailed guide also covers what to do if the county awards fewer hours than you need — including how to file for a state hearing and build an evidence-based appeal.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="before-you-apply">Before You Apply: Checklist</h2>

Do you have all of these? If not, get them in place first.

<ul class="plain-checklist">
  <li><label><input type="checkbox" disabled /> Your child has <b>active Medi-Cal</b> — call your county DHCS office to confirm</label></li>
  <li><label><input type="checkbox" disabled /> You know your <b>county IHSS office</b> — find it online or call 211</label></li>
  <li><label><input type="checkbox" disabled /> You understand what <b>"protective supervision" means</b> — it's constant, eyes-on watching to prevent danger (not general childcare or housework)</label></li>
  <li><label><input type="checkbox" disabled /> You can describe <b>specific examples</b> — think of 3–5 times in the past week your child was about to get hurt and you had to intervene</label></li>
  <li><label><input type="checkbox" disabled /> You have a sense of <b>how many hours per day</b> — roughly how many waking hours does your child need supervision?</label></li>
</ul>

Not ready yet? Talk to your <a href="/guides/regional-centers/">Regional Center</a> service coordinator — they can help guide you and answer questions before you apply.
