<PlainBox>
  "Institutional deeming" is a rule that lets a California child with a developmental
  disability get <b>Medi-Cal while living at home, with the parents' income not counted at
  all</b>. If you've been told your family "makes too much" for your child to get help, this
  is the door that's still open. It works through a waiver your Regional Center helps set up —
  and Medi-Cal is the key to IHSS and more.
</PlainBox>

<div class="cols">
<article class="prose">

<h2 class="qa-h" id="what-is-it">What is institutional deeming?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Normally, Medi-Cal counts parents' income when a child applies — "deeming" it to the child.
  Institutional deeming <b>turns that rule off</b>: under a Home and Community-Based Services
  (HCBS) waiver for people with developmental disabilities, your child is treated the way they
  would be if they lived in a medical facility — <b>only the child's own income and resources
  count</b> — while they keep living at home with you <Cite>[TBC: waiver authority]</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

The name is bureaucratic, so here is the plain version. Decades ago, a child who lived in an
institution qualified for Medicaid on their own income — which for most children is zero — while
the same child living at home was blocked by their parents' paychecks. Waivers were created to
fix that backwards incentive: they let the "institutional" counting rules follow the child
home. That's the whole trick, and it's why the rule feels too good to be true when a parent
first hears about it. It isn't. It's the system correcting itself.

The result: a family with a comfortable income — one that disqualifies the child from SSI and
from regular Medi-Cal — can still get the child full-scope Medi-Cal. And it isn't a lesser
version of coverage; it's Medi-Cal.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="who-qualifies">Does my child qualify?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  The path runs through your <b>Regional Center</b>: the waiver serves children with
  developmental disabilities who are Regional Center consumers, live at home, and meet the
  waiver's level-of-care criteria <Cite>[TBC: verify criteria]</Cite>. The child's <b>own</b>
  income and resources still count — but for most children that's little or nothing.
</AnswerFirst>

If your child is already a Regional Center consumer, you are most of the way there — ask your
service coordinator about institutional deeming directly, by that name. If your child isn't
through Regional Center intake yet, start there first: our
<a href="/guides/regional-centers/">Regional Center guide</a> walks the eligibility rules and
the decision clock, and the intake call is free regardless of income.

Two honest caveats while this page is in review: the exact level-of-care criteria, and how the
rule applies to children in Early Start only, are details your service coordinator can confirm
for your situation [TBC: verify both before publish]. What no one disputes is the headline:
parental income is not what decides this.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="how-to-apply">How do I apply, step by step?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Say these words to your Regional Center service coordinator: <i>"I'd like to pursue Medi-Cal
  through institutional deeming for my child."</i> The Regional Center handles the waiver side;
  a Medi-Cal application goes in for the child as their own household of one
  <Cite>[TBC: verify current application mechanics]</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

1. **Ask your service coordinator, in writing, to start institutional deeming.** Using the
   exact term routes the request correctly, and a written ask creates the paper trail this
   system responds to.
2. **File the child's Medi-Cal application.** Under deeming the child applies on their own
   income — answer income questions for the child, not the household [TBC: confirm how the
   application is flagged for deeming so the county doesn't count parental income by mistake].
3. **Watch for the county counting your income anyway.** It's the most common failure mode:
   an eligibility worker processes the application as a regular child case. If a denial cites
   parental income, don't accept it — reply that this is an institutional-deeming case through
   the Regional Center waiver, and loop your service coordinator in.
4. **Keep the award letter with your records.** Medi-Cal in hand is what unlocks the next
   doors — see below.

  "An eligibility worker told me flatly that we earned too much. She wasn't lying — she just
  didn't know this rule, because almost nobody does. The parents who get it are the parents
  who name it. You're allowed to know a rule the person across the counter doesn't."
</EmpathyCallout>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="what-it-unlocks">What does Medi-Cal unlock?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Medi-Cal is the eligibility key for <b>IHSS</b> — including
  <a href="/guides/benefits/ihss-protective-supervision/">protective supervision</a>, which can
  pay a parent for supervision hours — plus Medi-Cal-covered therapies, equipment, and
  services that private insurance denies or caps. This is why deeming is worth pursuing even
  if your child is already insured.
</AnswerFirst>

Many families use Medi-Cal as **secondary** coverage: private insurance stays primary, and
Medi-Cal picks up cost-sharing and fills gaps [TBC: verify secondary-coverage mechanics and
any premium/cost implications before publish]. And to retire the most stubborn myth in this
system one more time: your child does **not** need SSI to get IHSS — Medi-Cal is the
requirement, and deeming is a route to Medi-Cal that ignores your income entirely. The short
version lives at
<a href="/answers/does-my-child-need-ssi-to-get-ihss/">Does my child need SSI to get IHSS?</a>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="if-denied">What if the county says no?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Get the denial <b>in writing</b>, check whether it wrongly counted parental income, and know
  that Medi-Cal denials carry <b>appeal rights to a state hearing</b> — the deadline is printed
  on the notice itself <Cite>[TBC: verify appeal route + windows]</Cite>. Your Regional Center
  service coordinator can often fix a miscounted case without a hearing.
</AnswerFirst>

A denial that cites your income is, in a deeming case, usually a processing error rather than
a real decision — the fix is getting the case routed to someone who knows the waiver. Put the
correction request in writing, copy your service coordinator, and calendar the appeal deadline
from the notice as your backstop. If you want help beyond the Regional Center, the same free
advocacy resources in our <a href="/guides/regional-centers/">Regional Center guide</a> apply
here.
