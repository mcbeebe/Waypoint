<PlainBox>
  SSI (Supplemental Security Income) is a monthly federal payment for children with
  disabilities in lower-income households. Two things decide it: your child's
  <b> disability</b>, and your family's <b>income</b> — Social Security counts part of what
  parents earn using a formula called "deeming." In California, an SSI approval also brings
  <b> Medi-Cal automatically</b>. And if income rules your child out, you have lost less than
  you think — the biggest benefits don't require SSI at all.
</PlainBox>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="what-is-ssi">What is SSI — and what does it unlock?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  SSI pays a monthly benefit to a child who meets Social Security's <b>childhood disability
  standard</b> and whose household passes the <b>income and resource test</b>. In California
  the approval matters twice: the money itself, and <b>automatic Medi-Cal</b> that comes with
  it <Cite>[TBC: verify linkage]</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

The monthly amount runs up to the federal benefit rate — a figure that changes every January
with the cost-of-living adjustment [TBC: 2026 FBR pending verification against SSA.gov; this
page and the calculator update together when it's verified]. California adds a state
supplement on top for some recipients [TBC: verify current SSP treatment for children].
Households with countable income receive a reduced amount rather than all-or-nothing — which
is why running the numbers is worth it even when you assume you earn too much.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="disability-standard">Does my child meet the disability standard?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  For children, Social Security asks whether a physical or mental condition causes <b>"marked
  and severe functional limitations"</b> and has lasted, or is expected to last, at least
  <b> 12 months</b> <Cite>[TBC: verify standard]</Cite>. It is a functional test — how your
  child actually manages daily life — not a diagnosis checklist.
</AnswerFirst>

What that means practically: two children with the same diagnosis can get different answers,
because the question is limitation, not label. The evidence that carries the decision is the
record you likely already have — evaluations, IEP or IFSP documents, therapy notes, and your
own concrete account of hard days. Describe your child's hardest ordinary day, not their best
one; the standard is about what support they genuinely need.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="deeming">How does Social Security count our income?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Through <b>deeming</b>: SSA takes parents' income, subtracts allocations for each
  non-disabled child in the home and a living allowance for the parents, applies standard
  exclusions, and "deems" the remainder to your child <Cite>[TBC: POMS SI 01320]</Cite>. If
  the deemed amount is under the benefit rate, your child may qualify for a partial or full
  payment. Our <a href="/tools/ssi-deeming-calculator/">SSI deeming calculator</a> runs the
  estimate in a minute — nothing you type is stored.
</AnswerFirst>

Three things families consistently miss:

1. **Bigger households qualify at higher incomes.** Every non-disabled sibling at home
   shelters part of your income before deeming — families stop themselves with mental math
   that ignores this.
2. **Partial awards still carry full Medi-Cal.** In California, even a small monthly SSI
   amount brings the automatic Medi-Cal with it [TBC: verify].
3. **A "too much income" denial is not the end of the road** — it's the fork where the next
   guide begins. Keep reading.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="how-to-apply">How do I apply?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Start the application with Social Security — by phone or through SSA's child SSI process
  online <Cite>[TBC: verify current channels]</Cite> — and gather the record while you wait
  for the appointment: evaluations, school documents, provider lists, and your own written
  description of a typical hard day.
</AnswerFirst>

The process typically involves an interview about household income and a separate disability
review that requests records from your child's providers [TBC: verify current process
details]. Answer the income questions precisely, sign the releases so records actually flow,
and keep dated copies of everything you submit. If a decision goes against you, the notice
states your appeal rights and deadlines — calendar them the day it arrives [TBC: verify
appeal levels and windows before publish].

<EmpathyCallout label="Why this matters">
  Applying means writing your child's hardest moments down for a stranger, and that costs
  something. Do it anyway, and do it honestly. The form isn't the truth about your child —
  it's a key. Your child is the one teaching you what the good days look like.
</EmpathyCallout>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="if-denied">If income rules SSI out, what's left?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  The two biggest supports for a child with a developmental disability at home <b>don't
  require SSI</b>: Medi-Cal through
  <a href="/guides/benefits/medi-cal-institutional-deeming/">institutional deeming</a> — which
  ignores parental income entirely — and then
  <a href="/guides/benefits/ihss-protective-supervision/">IHSS protective supervision</a>,
  which requires Medi-Cal, not SSI.
</AnswerFirst>

That sequence — deeming, then IHSS — is the path most middle-income families actually walk,
and it's closed to no one by a paycheck. The myth that SSI is the gate stops more families
than any rule does; the short, shareable correction lives at
<a href="/answers/does-my-child-need-ssi-to-get-ihss/">Does my child need SSI to get
IHSS?</a> And Regional Center services were never income-tested in the first place — see the
<a href="/guides/regional-centers/">Regional Center guide</a>.
