<PlainBox>
  A Regional Center is a state-funded nonprofit that coordinates and pays for services for
  Californians with developmental disabilities — things like respite, therapies, and case
  management. Services are free to your family regardless of your income, because they're a
  right under California's Lanterman Act, not a benefit you have to be poor enough to receive.
</PlainBox>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="what-is-a-regional-center">What is a Regional Center?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Regional Centers are <b>21 private nonprofits funded by the State of California</b> to
  coordinate and fund services for people with developmental disabilities, under the Lanterman
  Act <Cite>WIC §4500 et seq.</Cite> Every Californian lives in exactly one Regional Center's
  territory, and <b>services are provided regardless of family income</b>.
</AnswerFirst>

Think of your Regional Center as two things at once: a **front door** and a **funder**. As a
front door, it's where your child gets evaluated and, if eligible, assigned a service
coordinator — a person whose job is to know your child's case and connect you to services. As
a funder, it pays for or arranges services that aren't available through other systems:
respite care, behavioral services, day programs, transportation, adaptive equipment, and more.

The Lanterman Act is unusual in American disability law. It doesn't say the state *may* help
your child — it establishes services as an entitlement for people who qualify. That's why
there's no income test, no waitlist-by-design, and no "we ran out of funding this year" as a
lawful answer. When you hear a parent say "the Regional Center is paying for that," this is
the system they're talking about.

Each of the 21 centers covers a specific geographic area. Which one serves you depends only on
where you live — not where your child goes to school or gets medical care. If you're not sure
which one is yours, our <a href="/tools/regional-center-finder/">Regional Center finder</a>
will tell you in a few seconds.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="is-my-child-eligible">Is my child eligible?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  For children 3 and older, eligibility requires a <b>developmental disability that began
  before age 18</b> and is a <b>"substantial disability"</b> — meaning significant functional
  limitations in <b>at least 3 of 7 major life areas</b> <Cite>WIC §4512</Cite>. Qualifying
  conditions include <b>autism, intellectual disability, cerebral palsy, and epilepsy</b>.
  Children <b>under 3</b> go through Early Start, which uses <b>broader criteria</b> — a
  diagnosis isn't required.
</AnswerFirst>

The seven life areas the Regional Center looks at are self-care, receptive and expressive
language, learning, mobility, self-direction, capacity for independent living, and economic
self-sufficiency. Your child doesn't need to struggle in all seven — significant limits in
three or more is the standard. For a young child, evaluators consider what's expected at that
age, so "economic self-sufficiency" isn't held against a 4-year-old.

The qualifying conditions are autism, intellectual disability, cerebral palsy, epilepsy, and
"other conditions closely related to intellectual disability or requiring similar treatment."
A diagnosis alone doesn't guarantee eligibility, and the absence of one of these exact labels
doesn't automatically disqualify — the substantial-disability analysis is what matters. That
fifth category exists precisely for children whose needs are real but whose diagnosis doesn't
fit a neat box.

If your child is **under 3**, stop reading the fine print and call now. Early Start serves
infants and toddlers with developmental delays or risk conditions using intentionally broader
criteria than the over-3 standard, and the decision clock is faster — 45 days instead of 120
<Cite>WIC §4643</Cite>. Some children who qualify for Early Start won't qualify at age 3, and
that's okay: two-plus years of early intervention is worth having either way.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="how-do-i-apply">How do I apply — and how long can it take?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Call your Regional Center's <b>intake line</b> and say: <i>"I'd like to request an intake and
  assessment for my child for Regional Center eligibility."</i> Once intake begins, the center
  must make an eligibility decision <b>within 120 days</b> — or <b>within 45 days</b> for a
  child under 3 going through Early Start <Cite>WIC §4643</Cite>. You do not need a doctor's
  referral, a lawyer, or a completed diagnosis to start.
</AnswerFirst>

That phone call is the whole secret to starting. Use the words "intake and assessment" —
they're the formal trigger. The intake worker will ask about your child's development, your
concerns, and your address (to confirm you're in their catchment area). Ask them to confirm,
in writing if possible, the date your intake request was received: that's the date the clock
starts.

Gather what you have, but don't wait until your folder is perfect: any diagnostic reports or
medical records, school or daycare notes, IEP or IFSP documents if they exist, and your own
written list of what your child struggles with day to day. The Regional Center will do its own
assessments — psychological, developmental, adaptive — at no cost to you. Your job is to paint
an honest picture of the hard days, not the best days.

If the clock passes with no decision, you don't have to wait politely. Put it in writing: name
the date intake began, cite <Cite>WIC §4643</Cite>, and ask for a decision date. Deadlines in
this system are real, and centers respond to parents who know them.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="what-happens-at-the-ipp">What happens at the IPP meeting?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  The IPP — <b>Individual Program Plan</b> — is the written plan that drives everything the
  Regional Center funds for your child <Cite>WIC §4646</Cite>. It's built at a meeting between
  you and your service coordinator, and it's meant to be a <b>collaboration, not a
  presentation</b>. The key strategy: <b>services flow from goals</b>, so come with your
  child's goals — and your asks — in writing.
</AnswerFirst>

Here's the mechanic most parents learn too late: the Regional Center doesn't fund services in
the abstract. It funds services that support written goals in the IPP. If the plan says "Mateo
will communicate wants and needs using 3-word phrases," then speech services, a communication
device, and parent coaching all have a hook to hang on. No goal, no hook. So before the
meeting, write down what you want your child's life to look like in a year — then work
backward to the supports that get there.

Bring a one-page list of your asks and hand it over at the start. Being in writing does two
things: it keeps the meeting on your agenda, and it creates a record. If a request is
declined, ask for the response in writing too — that's what triggers your appeal rights (more
on that below). You can bring anyone you want for support: a family member, a friend, an
advocate.

The IPP isn't carved in stone. You can request a new IPP meeting when circumstances change — a
new diagnosis, a new school situation, a family crisis. You don't have to wait for the annual
review to ask for more.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="what-if-they-say-no">What if they say no?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  A denial — of eligibility or of a specific service — must come as a written <b>Notice of
  Action (NOA)</b> that explains the decision and your <b>appeal rights</b>. Appeal deadlines
  are short and strict — on the order of 30 days, and sooner if you want services to continue
  during the appeal [TBC: verify current appeal windows; state the dates printed on the NOA,
  not from memory]. A "no" on the phone is not a decision. Ask for the NOA.
</AnswerFirst>

That last point deserves repeating, because it's where many families get stuck: an informal
no — a coordinator saying "we don't fund that" or "your child probably won't qualify" — has no
legal weight and starts no clock. Politely insist: *"Please send me that decision as a written
Notice of Action."* The NOA is what converts a shrug into something you can appeal.

From the NOA, the path runs through an appeal to a **fair hearing** before an independent
judge, usually with an optional informal meeting or mediation first — many disputes settle
before a hearing ever happens. Read the deadlines printed on your own NOA carefully and
calendar them the day it arrives: filing on time is what preserves your rights, and filing
quickly can keep existing services in place while the appeal is decided [TBC: confirm the
aid-paid-pending window before publish].

You don't have to do this alone, and you don't have to pay anyone. The **Office of Clients'
Rights Advocacy (OCRA)** — part of **Disability Rights California** — provides free advocates
who know the Lanterman Act and handle Regional Center appeals for families like yours. Denials
get reversed regularly, especially when a parent shows up with the statute, the record, and an
advocate. A no today is often a not-yet.
