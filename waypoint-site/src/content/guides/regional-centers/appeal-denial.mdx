<PlainBox>
  A Regional Center "no" only counts — and only starts your appeal — once it is in writing, on a
  form called a Notice of Action. So the first thing to do is ask for one. From the day you get
  it, you have <b>60 days</b> to appeal. If a service you already have is being cut and you want
  it to keep running while you argue, you have <b>30 days</b>, and you must file before the
  change takes effect. Appealing is free. Free advocates will do it with you.
</PlainBox>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="what-is-an-noa">What is a Notice of Action?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  A <b>Notice of Action (NOA)</b> is the written decision a Regional Center must send when it
  denies, reduces, ends, or changes services — or decides your child isn't eligible. California
  law spells out what it has to say: the action, the facts behind it, the reasons, the effective
  date, <b>the specific law, regulation, or policy the center is relying on</b>, how to appeal,
  and where to get free advocacy help <Cite>WIC §4701</Cite>. It is the document that turns a
  "no" into something you can appeal.
</AnswerFirst>

Read that list again, because it is doing more work than it looks like. An NOA cannot say "we
don't fund that." It has to name the provision of law, regulation, or policy that supports the
decision <Cite>WIC §4701</Cite>. Many families discover, the moment they read that box, that the
stated reason is a Regional Center *policy* rather than a statute — which is a very different
thing to argue about, and a much more winnable one.

The NOA must also tell you how to file an appeal, what your rights are during it (including an
interpreter and access to your child's records), and that free advocacy help exists, with
referrals <Cite>WIC §4701</Cite>. If the notice you're holding is missing pieces, say so in your
appeal. A notice that doesn't meet the statute is itself a problem.

There are timing rules on the center's side, too. Before it reduces, ends, or changes a service
in your child's Individual Program Plan — or decides your child is no longer eligible — the
center must give you at least **30 days' advance notice** <Cite>WIC §4710</Cite>. When it denies
a service you asked for, it must send notice **within 5 business days** of making that decision
<Cite>WIC §4710</Cite>. And when a change has to happen immediately for someone's health and
safety, the notice still has to arrive within 10 days after <Cite>WIC §4710</Cite>.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="get-it-in-writing">Why is "get it in writing" the most important thing on this page?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Because a verbal "no" starts no clock, creates no record, and cannot be appealed. Your appeal
  rights attach to the <b>Notice of Action</b> — the 60-day and 30-day windows both run from when
  you receive it <Cite>WIC §4710.5</Cite> <Cite>WIC §4715</Cite>. Until an NOA exists, there is
  nothing to appeal. So when you hear no, ask one question: <i>"Will you please send me that
  decision as a written Notice of Action?"</i>
</AnswerFirst>

This is where more families lose services than at any hearing. The "no" almost never arrives as
a formal denial. It arrives as a service coordinator saying the center doesn't fund that, or that
your child probably wouldn't qualify, or that the budget for respite is committed this year, or
simply as a request that goes unanswered for three months. None of that is a decision in the
legal sense. It is also, functionally, a denial — your child doesn't get the service either way.

So convert it. Politely, in writing, the same day:

*"Thanks for talking this through with me today. So I understand the decision correctly — you're
declining to fund the 20 hours of respite I asked for at Mateo's IPP meeting on March 3. Could
you please send me a written Notice of Action for that decision? I'd like to see the reasons and
the provision it's based on. Thank you."*

Three things that email does. It creates a dated record that you asked. It tells a coordinator
who may have been speaking casually that this is now a formal decision, which sometimes changes
the answer on its own. And if the NOA does come, you have exactly what the statute requires it to
contain — including the legal basis you'll be arguing about <Cite>WIC §4701</Cite>.

If no NOA arrives, follow up in writing and name the deadline: a denial of a requested service is
supposed to be noticed within 5 business days of the decision <Cite>WIC §4710</Cite>. Copy your
service coordinator's supervisor. And call an advocate — see below — because a center that won't
issue a notice is a center trying to keep you out of a process you are entitled to.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="deadlines">How long do I have to appeal?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  <b>60 days</b> from notification of the decision to file an appeal <Cite>WIC §4710.5</Cite>.
  But if services you already receive are being reduced or ended and you want them to
  <b>keep running during the appeal</b>, your request must be postmarked or received within
  <b>30 days</b> of receiving the notice <b>and</b> before the action's effective date
  <Cite>WIC §4715</Cite>. Those are two different clocks doing two different jobs. Check the
  dates printed on your own NOA the day it arrives.
</AnswerFirst>

<DeadlineCallout>
  <b>60 days</b> to appeal, from notification of the decision <Cite>WIC §4710.5</Cite>. ·
  <b>30 days</b> — and before the effective date — to keep current services running while the
  appeal is pending <Cite>WIC §4715</Cite>. These rules took their current form on
  <b>March 1, 2023</b> <Cite>WIC §4710.5</Cite>; older guides and older parents may still tell
  you a different number.
</DeadlineCallout>

That March 2023 note matters. California rewrote this chapter, and a great deal of advice still
circulating online — including from people who genuinely helped families for years — describes
the previous rules. If a number you read somewhere doesn't match the number on your NOA, trust
your NOA and the statute.

Here's the plain way to think about the two clocks. The **60-day** window is the one that decides
whether you get to appeal at all. The **30-day** window is the one that decides whether your
child keeps their current hours while you do. If your child has services now and the center is
taking them away, treat 30 days as your real deadline — and file before the date the notice says
the change takes effect, because both conditions have to be met <Cite>WIC §4715</Cite>.

When continuation applies, services keep running through the **10th day after** the appeal ends —
after you withdraw, after an informal-meeting decision stands, after mediation closes, or after
you receive the final hearing or reconsideration decision <Cite>WIC §4715</Cite>. That tail gives
you a short window to plan rather than losing the service the same afternoon.

One thing worth saying plainly: continuation is not a reason to file a weak appeal, and it is not
a trick. It is a protection the Legislature built so a family isn't forced to absorb a cut before
anyone has looked at whether the cut was correct.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="how-to-file">How do I actually file the appeal?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  You file an <b>Appeal Request form (DS 1821)</b> with the Department of Developmental Services
  — not with the Regional Center. Your NOA comes with the form, and DDS also takes appeal
  requests online, by email, by fax, and by mail. On the form you choose which of the three
  processes you want: <b>informal meeting, mediation, fair hearing</b> — one, two, or all three
  <Cite>WIC §4707</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

Send it the way you can prove. If you mail it, the postmark is what counts for the 30-day
continuation rule <Cite>WIC §4715</Cite>, so keep the receipt. If you email or file online, keep
the confirmation. Photograph the completed form before it leaves your hands.

What to write in the "reason" box: keep it short and factual. Name the service, name the date of
the NOA, and say in one or two sentences why the decision is wrong — what your child needs and
what the record shows. You are not writing a brief. You are opening a door. You will have the
informal meeting, the mediation, or the hearing to explain the rest, and you can add evidence
later.

Do not wait until you have the perfect argument, the doctor's letter, or a call back from an
advocate. File within the window with what you have. An appeal filed on time and thin is
recoverable; an appeal filed late usually is not.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="three-paths">What are the three ways to appeal?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  <b>An informal meeting</b> with the Regional Center — held within <b>10 days</b>, with a written
  decision within <b>5 days</b> after <Cite>WIC §4710.6</Cite> <Cite>WIC §4710.7</Cite>.
  <b>Mediation</b> with a neutral mediator — held within <b>30 days</b>
  <Cite>WIC §4711.5</Cite>. And a <b>fair hearing</b> before an independent hearing officer —
  held within <b>50 days</b>, decided within <b>80 days</b> of your request
  <Cite>WIC §4712</Cite> <Cite>WIC §4712.5</Cite>. You pick one or more on the form
  <Cite>WIC §4707</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

**The informal meeting** is the cheapest thing you will ever do in this system. It is a
conversation with the center's director or their designee, scheduled at a mutually agreed time
within 10 days of the department receiving your request <Cite>WIC §4710.6</Cite>, and you get the
outcome in writing within 5 days of the meeting <Cite>WIC §4710.7</Cite>. A surprising share of
denials die here — because the person across from you actually reads the file, or because a
document nobody had was sitting in your kitchen drawer. Bring the records. Bring one page of what
you want.

**Mediation** is a neutral, non-binding conversation aimed at an agreement. The mediator must be
familiar with the Lanterman Act and must not be in the business of providing or supervising
services for regional centers <Cite>WIC §4711.5</Cite>. They may meet with each side separately
and speak confidentially with either of you to help find a resolution <Cite>WIC §4711.5</Cite>.
Either party may withdraw after the first session and go on to a fair hearing
<Cite>WIC §4711.5</Cite> — so trying mediation does not trap you.

**The fair hearing** is the binding one. It's held within 50 days of your request unless you ask
for a continuance <Cite>WIC §4712</Cite>. Any relevant evidence is admitted, both sides put
documents in at the start, and position statements and witness lists are exchanged at least two
business days beforehand <Cite>WIC §4712</Cite>. If English isn't your preferred language, the
hearing office provides an interpreter <Cite>WIC §4712</Cite>. The hearing officer has legal
training and no connection to your Regional Center or financial interest in the outcome
<Cite>WIC §4712</Cite>.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="lawyers">Will the Regional Center bring a lawyer?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Usually it cannot. A Regional Center or department attorney <b>may not attend</b> an informal
  meeting, mediation, or fair hearing unless you are yourself an attorney or you will have one
  present <Cite>WIC §4705.5</Cite>. If you do bring counsel, you tell the center and the hearing
  office <b>5 days</b> before mediation or <b>15 days</b> before a fair hearing
  <Cite>WIC §4705.5</Cite>. A hearing officer may allow an attorney "in the interest of justice,"
  with advance notice to both sides <Cite>WIC §4705.5</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

This rule exists to keep the table level, and it is one of the best things about the Lanterman
appeal process. You are not walking into a room where a public agency's lawyer questions you
while you sit alone. If you go without counsel, they go without counsel.

That does not mean you should go alone. You may be represented by an advocate or another
supporter at the hearing <Cite>WIC §4712</Cite>, and a free, experienced advocate is very
different from an attorney for the purposes of this rule. Which brings us to the part of this
page most worth acting on today.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="free-help">Who will help me for free?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  The <b>Office of Clients' Rights Advocacy (OCRA)</b>, part of <b>Disability Rights
  California</b>, provides free advice and representation to Regional Center clients across all
  21 centers, including in meetings and hearings. Your Notice of Action is required to tell you
  that free advocacy exists and to give you referrals <Cite>WIC §4701</Cite>. Call before you
  file if you can — but file on time either way.
</AnswerFirst>

OCRA advocates do this work every week. They know which arguments move a particular center, what
a hearing officer wants to see, and how to read the "legal basis" box on your NOA. Their help
costs nothing, and using it is not an escalation — it is the ordinary way this process is meant
to run.

You can reach OCRA through
<a href="https://www.disabilityrightsca.org/what-we-do/programs/office-of-clients-rights-advocacy-ocra">Disability Rights California's OCRA page</a>,
which lists local offices by region. Disability Rights California is California's federally
mandated protection and advocacy organization. We link to them as a resource; the law on this
page comes from the statute, so you can check us against it.

Two other free supports worth knowing: your Regional Center's own clients' rights advocate,
whose contact information the center must post, and California's Family Resource Centers and
Family Empowerment Centers, which help parents prepare for meetings.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="if-you-lose">What if the hearing officer rules against me?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  The fair hearing decision is the final administrative decision and binds both sides
  <Cite>WIC §4712.5</Cite>. You have two moves left. Within <b>15 days</b> of the final decision,
  you may ask for <b>reconsideration</b> — a correction of a mistake of fact, law, or a clerical
  error <Cite>WIC §4713</Cite>. And within <b>180 days</b> of receiving the final decision, either
  party may appeal to a court <Cite>WIC §4712.5</Cite>. Reconsideration is optional; it is not a
  prerequisite for going to court <Cite>WIC §4713</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

Reconsideration is narrow by design. It is for "we got a date wrong," "the decision applied the
wrong section," or "the officer overlooked the evaluation in the record" — not for "I disagree."
If that's your situation, it is fast and free, and services continue unchanged while it is
reviewed <Cite>WIC §4713</Cite>.

Court is a different order of commitment. Going to a court of competent jurisdiction within 180
days <Cite>WIC §4712.5</Cite> means retaining counsel, and that is the point on this ladder where
a lawyer stops being optional. Talk to OCRA or Disability Rights California first; they will tell
you honestly whether your record supports it.

There is also a quieter option that families forget. A denial decided on today's facts is not
permanent. You can request a new Individual Program Plan meeting when circumstances change — a
new diagnosis, a new evaluation, a crisis, a school change — and ask again with new evidence. A
good independent evaluation, a physician's letter, or a documented month of what your family's
days actually look like changes the case. Read
<GatedLink href="/guides/regional-centers/ipp-meeting-prep/">how to prepare for an IPP
meeting</GatedLink> and come back to that table with the record you wish you'd had.

And if your child is also in special education, the school system has its own, entirely separate
ladder with different deadlines and different forms — see
<GatedLink href="/guides/iep/dispute-ladder/">the IEP dispute ladder</GatedLink>. Winning one
does not decide the other, and a service denied by one system is sometimes owed by the other.

<EmpathyCallout label="From a parent who's been there">
  "The letter said 'Notice of Action' in bold and I put it face-down on the counter for four days
  because I couldn't look at it. When I finally read it, the reason they gave was one sentence
  long, and it was wrong — they'd used an old assessment. I filed the appeal on day nine with a
  paragraph and a phone photo of the newer report. We never got to a hearing. The informal meeting
  fixed it in twenty minutes. Four of my nine days were spent being afraid of an envelope."
</EmpathyCallout>

A denial is not a verdict on your child, and it is not the end of the conversation. It is the
start of a process the Legislature wrote down, with clocks that run on the Regional Center as
well as on you, a hearing officer who does not work for them, a rule that keeps their lawyer out
of the room unless you bring one, and free advocates whose entire job is this. You asked for the
decision in writing, you read the reasons, and you calendared the date. That is exactly what a
parent who knows the system does. You did this.
