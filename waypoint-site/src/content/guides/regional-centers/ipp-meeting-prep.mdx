<PlainBox>
  The IPP is the written plan that decides what your Regional Center pays for. Services hang
  on goals, so the goals you bring in writing become the services you get. The law says the
  plan is built <b>by agreement</b> between you and the Regional Center — you are not there to
  be told the answer. Before you leave the meeting, you should walk out with a written, signed
  list of what was agreed to. If something is refused, ask for it in writing.
</PlainBox>

<div class="cols">
<article class="prose">

Your meeting is tomorrow, or next week, and you have a knot in your stomach about it. That
knot is not a sign you're unprepared — it's a sign you understand the stakes. Here is
everything that meeting actually is, what the law requires it to produce, and the exact words
that work in the room. Work the checklist at the bottom tonight and you will walk in with
more preparation than most people at the table.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="what-is-an-ipp">What is an IPP, and what must it contain?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  The <b>Individual Program Plan (IPP)</b> is the written plan the Regional Center builds with
  you, and it is what every funded service attaches to <Cite>WIC §4646</Cite>. It must be
  <b>prepared jointly by the planning team</b>, with decisions about goals and services
  <b>made by agreement</b> between the Regional Center and you — not announced to you. By law
  the plan must contain <b>measurable goals and time-limited objectives</b>, plus a
  <b>schedule of the type and amount of services</b>, who provides each one, and an
  approximate start date <Cite>WIC §4646.5</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

That last requirement is the one to hold onto. The statute does not let the plan say "speech
services as needed." It calls for the type *and the amount* of each service, the provider
responsible for it, and roughly when it starts <Cite>WIC §4646.5</Cite>. If a draft plan comes
back vague, vagueness is not the Regional Center's style — it's a gap in what the law asks the
plan to say, and you can ask for the number.

Here is what the planning process has to include, in the order the statute lists it:

<div class="table-wrap">
  <table class="data">
    <tr><th>The law requires</th><th>What that means at your table</th></tr>
    <tr><td>Assessments of your child's goals, strengths, preferences, and barriers — and, for a child, a look at the family unit as a whole <span class="cite">WIC §4646.5(a)(1)</span></td><td>Your description of a normal Tuesday is evidence. Assessments are to be done in natural settings where possible.</td></tr>
    <tr><td>A statement of goals, plus <b>specific, time-limited objectives</b> "stated in terms that allow measurement of progress" <span class="cite">WIC §4646.5(a)(2)</span></td><td>Fuzzy goals produce fuzzy services. Bring goals already written this way.</td></tr>
    <tr><td>A schedule of the <b>type and amount</b> of services and supports, who provides each, and the approximate start date <span class="cite">WIC §4646.5(a)(5)</span></td><td>Hours per week and a start date belong in the plan, not in someone's inbox.</td></tr>
    <tr><td>A schedule of regular review to check that services were actually delivered <span class="cite">WIC §4646.5(a)(8)</span></td><td>The plan includes its own follow-up. Put the review date in your calendar.</td></tr>
    <tr><td>A written or electronic <b>list of the agreed-upon services</b>, signed by the Regional Center at the end of the meeting <span class="cite">WIC §4646(g)</span></td><td>This is your receipt. Do not leave without it.</td></tr>
  </table>
</div>

Three rights most parents are never told about, all in the same section. You can ask for the
meeting to be held **by remote electronic communications**, and the Regional Center must hold
it that way if you request it <Cite>WIC §4646(f)(1)</Cite>. If there has been no in-person
meeting or visit in the past 12 months, the center must hold one **at a time and place
convenient for you**, reflecting your preference <Cite>WIC §4646(f)(2)</Cite>. And the whole
planning process — the meeting, the copies of the plan, the list of services — happens **in
your preferred language**, with your preferred language written into the IPP itself
<Cite>WIC §4646(j)</Cite>. You do not have to bring your own interpreter, and you should not
have to use your older child as one.

<DeadlineCallout>
  After your child is found eligible and the assessment is done, the IPP must be
  <b>completed within 60 days of the completion of the assessment</b> <span class="cite">WIC
  §4646(c)</span>. Separately, once the plan exists, you can ask for a review any time — the
  Regional Center must review it <b>within 30 days</b> of your request, or <b>within 7 days</b>
  if your child's health and safety or staying in your home depends on it
  <span class="cite">WIC §4646.5(b)</span>. You never have to wait for the annual meeting to
  ask.
</DeadlineCallout>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="write-goals">How do I write goals the Regional Center can fund?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Write goals in the form the statute already asks for: a plain-English goal, then a
  <b>specific, time-limited objective you could measure</b> <Cite>WIC §4646.5(a)(2)</Cite>.
  Then, under each one, name the support that gets your child there. Services attach to
  written goals — so a need that never becomes a goal has nothing to hang on. Bring the whole
  thing on <b>one page</b> and hand a copy to everyone at the table at the start.
</AnswerFirst>

The mechanic parents learn too late: the Regional Center does not fund services in the
abstract. It funds services that carry out goals written in the plan. So the drafting work you
do at your kitchen table the night before is the highest-leverage hour in the whole process.

Use three columns and keep it to a page:

<div class="table-wrap">
  <table class="data">
    <tr><th>Goal (what life looks like in a year)</th><th>Objective (measurable, with a date)</th><th>Support that gets us there</th></tr>
    <tr><td>Ana asks for what she wants instead of melting down</td><td>By June, Ana uses 3-word phrases or her device to request in 4 out of 5 daily opportunities</td><td>Speech services; a communication device evaluation; parent coaching</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Our family can get through a week without running on empty</td><td>Starting next month, a trained provider covers 20 hours a month so we sleep and our other child gets a Saturday</td><td>Respite hours <span class="cite">WIC §4512(b)</span></td></tr>
    <tr><td>Marcus gets to school safely and on time</td><td>By the start of the next term, Marcus travels to his program 5 days a week with support</td><td>Transportation, named provider, start date <span class="cite">WIC §4646.5(a)(5)</span></td></tr>
  </table>
</div>

Two drafting habits that change outcomes. **Write the hard days, not the good ones.** The plan
is built on your child's needs as described, and the version of your child a stranger sees for
90 minutes on a good morning is not the version you live with. **Put a number on everything.**
"More respite" is a wish; "20 hours a month, starting in October" is an objective someone can
schedule, fund, and be held to.

<EmpathyCallout label="If writing this feels like betrayal">
  Listing everything your child struggles with, in writing, for strangers, can feel like you're
  filing a complaint about a person you love more than anything. It isn't. You are describing
  the size of the gap between the world as it is built and the child you have, so that someone
  is required to help close it. Write the hardest week you had this year. You are not
  disloyal — you are being accurate on purpose, which is much harder.
</EmpathyCallout>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="scripts-asking">What do I actually say to ask for a service?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Ask in three moves: name the <b>need</b>, name the <b>goal it serves</b>, then name the
  <b>service and the amount</b>. Ask for the goal to be written into the plan even if funding
  is still being discussed — the goal is the hook the service hangs on. Then ask for the
  service, the amount, the provider, and the start date to be in the plan, which is what
  <Cite>WIC §4646.5(a)(5)</Cite> requires it to say.
</AnswerFirst>

Say it out loud once before you go. These are meant to be read almost word for word.

**To open the meeting:**

*"Before we start, I've written down my goals for Ana this year and what I think it takes to
get there. Here's a copy for everyone. Can we use it as our agenda?"*

**To ask for a service:**

*"Ana can't tell us what she needs yet, and that's where most of our hard days start. So my
goal is that she can request things in 3-word phrases or on a device by June. To get there I'm
asking for speech services twice a week and a communication device evaluation. Can we write
that goal into the IPP, with the service, the amount, and a start date?"*

**To get respite on the table without apologizing for it:**

*"I'm asking for 20 hours of respite a month. It's not a luxury — it's what keeps Ana at home
with us. Can we add a goal about our family sustaining care at home, and put respite hours
under it?"*

**When someone says a decision has to wait:**

*"That's fine. Can we write the goal in today anyway, and note that the service under it is
still being decided? I'd rather not lose the goal while we wait."*

**Before anyone stands up — the most important thirty seconds of the meeting:**

*"Can I get the written list of what we agreed to today before we finish, with the start dates
and the providers?"*

You are entitled to ask for exactly that. At the conclusion of an IPP meeting, the Regional
Center's authorized representative must give you — on paper or electronically — a **list of
the agreed-upon services and supports**, with the projected start date if known, the frequency
and duration, and the provider, and must **sign it at that time** <Cite>WIC §4646(g)</Cite>.
That signed list is the difference between an agreement and a memory.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="scripts-no">What do I say when they say "we don't fund that"?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Don't argue and don't agree. Ask <b>three questions</b>: what is the reason, is it written
  anywhere, and will you put it in writing as a <b>Notice of Action</b>? An informal "we don't
  fund that" is not a decision and starts no clock. Also ask the generic-resource question —
  if a medical service in the plan <b>isn't available within 60 calendar days</b> through your
  insurance or Medi-Cal, the planning team can decide the Regional Center funds it
  <Cite>WIC §4646.4(a)(2)</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

"We don't fund that" usually means one of four very different things, and your next sentence
depends on which:

<div class="table-wrap">
  <table class="data">
    <tr><th>What they may mean</th><th>What to say</th></tr>
    <tr><td>"Your insurance or Medi-Cal covers it" (the generic-resource rule)</td><td><i>"I've asked. Can we document that it isn't available within 60 calendar days through my plan? My understanding is the team can authorize it then."</i> <span class="cite">WIC §4646.4(a)(2)</span></td></tr>
    <tr><td>"It's the school's job"</td><td><i>"The school covers school hours. This is a need at home, outside the school day. Can we address that need here?"</i></td></tr>
    <tr><td>"It's not in our purchase-of-service policy"</td><td><i>"Can you send me the policy section you're relying on? And can we still record my request and your answer in the plan?"</i></td></tr>
    <tr><td>"There's no funding this year"</td><td><i>"I understand the budget is hard. My child's services under the Lanterman Act aren't a budget question, though. Can I have that answer as a written Notice of Action?"</i></td></tr>
  </table>
</div>

Then, whatever the reason, use the sentence that converts a shrug into something you can act
on:

*"I hear you. Please send me that decision as a written Notice of Action so I can look at my
options."*

Say it kindly. Most service coordinators are not your adversary — they are carrying 70 other
families and an internal policy nobody explained to them either. But say it every time,
because the written notice is what turns a hallway conversation into a decision with a clock
and an appeal attached.

One more thing you can do without any conflict at all: if the meeting ends without agreement,
you don't have to sign in defeat and you don't have to storm out. **A second meeting must be
convened within 15 days** when a final agreement can't be reached <Cite>WIC §4646(h)</Cite>.
Ask for it before you leave.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="if-refused">What if something is refused?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Get the <b>Notice of Action (NOA)</b> — the written decision. You can also <b>sign the IPP
  and mark your disagreement</b> with the parts you don't accept: the services you did agree on
  still start, and your disagreement is on the record <Cite>WIC §4646(i)</Cite>. From the NOA,
  you have <b>60 days from notification</b> to ask for an informal meeting, mediation, or a
  fair hearing <Cite>WIC §4710.5</Cite>. Denials get reversed regularly.
</AnswerFirst>

Signing with a noted disagreement is the move most parents don't know exists. Disagreeing with
specific parts of the plan does not stop the parts you agreed on from being implemented, and
if you don't agree in whole or in part, the Regional Center owes you **written notice of your
appeal rights** <Cite>WIC §4646(i)</Cite>. So you never have to choose between accepting
everything and delaying the services your child needs now.

<DeadlineCallout>
  <b>60 days from notification</b> of the decision to file for an informal meeting, mediation,
  or a fair hearing <span class="cite">WIC §4710.5(a)</span>. A different, shorter clock
  applies if you want a service to keep running while you appeal — check the dates printed on
  your own notice, and see the appeal guide below.
</DeadlineCallout>

The request goes on the state's appeal request form, and here is a detail worth knowing: if
you ask any Regional Center employee to appeal in some other way, that employee is required to
give you the form and to **help you fill it out** if you want help <Cite>WIC §4710.5(c)</Cite>.
Asking for help with the form is not a favor you're begging for.

Our full walkthrough of that path — reading the NOA, the deadlines, the informal meeting,
mediation, and the fair hearing — is in
<GatedLink href="/guides/regional-centers/appeal-denial/">how to appeal a Regional Center denial</GatedLink>.
Free help exists too: the **Office of Clients' Rights Advocacy (OCRA)**, part of Disability
Rights California, represents families in Regional Center appeals at no cost. At intake, your
Regional Center was required to give you the address and phone number of the state council and
the protection and advocacy agency <Cite>WIC §4646(c)</Cite> — if that never happened, ask for
it now.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="checklist">The night-before checklist</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Work these in order. Everything above the meeting takes about an hour at your kitchen table;
  everything below it takes five minutes in the room. Print this page, or check the boxes here
  and carry them into your Waypoint plan.
</AnswerFirst>

<PhaseLabel>Two weeks out · Set the meeting up to work</PhaseLabel>

<CheckItem id="ipp-confirm-logistics" title="Confirm the date, and ask for the format you actually need">
  You can require the meeting be held <b>by remote electronic communications</b>
  <span class="cite">WIC §4646(f)(1)</span>, and if there's been no in-person meeting or visit
  in the last 12 months you can require an in-person one at a time and place convenient for you
  <span class="cite">WIC §4646(f)(2)</span>. Ask in the same email that confirms the date.
</CheckItem>

<CheckItem id="ipp-language-access" title="Ask for your preferred language in writing">
  The Regional Center must run the planning process — the meeting and the documents — in your
  preferred language, and must write that language into the IPP
  <span class="cite">WIC §4646(j)</span>. Ask now, not on the day, so an interpreter is booked.
</CheckItem>

<CheckItem id="ipp-request-draft" title="Ask for the draft plan and any assessments in advance">
  Ask your service coordinator to email you the current IPP and any assessment reports the team
  will rely on. Reading them cold in the meeting is how good asks get lost.
</CheckItem>

<CheckItem id="ipp-invite-someone" title="Decide who comes with you">
  You can bring anyone: your partner, a friend, a relative who takes notes, or a free advocate
  from OCRA. Two people hear twice as much, and one of them can watch the clock while the other
  talks.
</CheckItem>

<PhaseLabel>The night before · One hour at the kitchen table</PhaseLabel>

<CheckItem id="ipp-write-goals" title="Write your one-page goals sheet">
  Three columns: goal, measurable objective with a date, and the support that gets you there —
  the shape <span class="cite">WIC §4646.5(a)(2)</span> asks for. Keep it to one page and print
  a copy for everyone at the table.
</CheckItem>

<CheckItem id="ipp-hard-week" title="Write down your hardest week, honestly">
  Specific beats general every time: the elopement out the front door, the 3am wakings, the
  meltdown at pickup, the number of times you left work early last month. This is the evidence
  behind every number you're about to ask for.
</CheckItem>

<CheckItem id="ipp-name-amounts" title="Put a number on every ask">
  Hours per week or per month, and a start date. The plan is required to state the type
  <b>and amount</b> of each service and roughly when it begins
  <span class="cite">WIC §4646.5(a)(5)</span> — so bring the numbers you want it to state.
</CheckItem>

<CheckItem id="ipp-gather-records" title="Gather the paper — but don't wait for a perfect folder">
  Recent evaluations, doctor's notes, the current IEP or IFSP, any denial letters from
  insurance, and your supervision or incident log if you keep one. Bring what you have.
</CheckItem>

<PhaseLabel>In the room · Five minutes that decide the year</PhaseLabel>

<CheckItem id="ipp-hand-out-sheet" title="Hand out your goals sheet in the first two minutes">
  It sets the agenda, and it creates a record that these asks were made on this date. Say:
  <i>"Can we use this as our agenda?"</i>
</CheckItem>

<CheckItem id="ipp-ask-and-name-amount" title="Make each ask as need → goal → service and amount">
  Say the need, say the goal it serves, then name the service, the amount, the provider, and
  the start date you want written down. Decisions are made <b>by agreement</b> between you and
  the Regional Center <span class="cite">WIC §4646(d)</span> — you are a decision-maker at that
  table, not an audience.
</CheckItem>

<CheckItem id="ipp-noa-on-refusal" title="If anything is refused, ask for it in writing">
  <i>"Please send me that decision as a written Notice of Action."</i> An informal no starts no
  clock; the NOA starts your 60 days <span class="cite">WIC §4710.5(a)</span>.
</CheckItem>

<CheckItem id="ipp-get-signed-list" title="Do not leave without the signed list of agreed-upon services">
  At the conclusion of the meeting the Regional Center must give you, in writing or
  electronically, the list of agreed-upon services with start dates, frequency, duration, and
  provider — signed by their representative right then
  <span class="cite">WIC §4646(g)</span>. If no agreement was reached, ask for the second
  meeting, which must be convened within 15 days <span class="cite">WIC §4646(h)</span>.
</CheckItem>

<CheckItem id="ipp-calendar-followups" title="Calendar the start dates and the review date before you forget">
  Every start date on that list is a promise with a date attached. Put each one in your phone,
  plus the plan's own review date <span class="cite">WIC §4646.5(a)(8)</span>. When a start
  date passes with nothing happening, email your coordinator that week.
</CheckItem>

<CheckItem id="ipp-followup-email" title="Send a short thank-you email that repeats what was agreed">
  Two sentences: thank them, and list what was agreed with the dates. It's gracious, and it's a
  second written record that costs you three minutes.
</CheckItem>
