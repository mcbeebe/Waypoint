<PlainBox>
  California law makes most health plans cover the care a doctor says your child needs for a
  mental health or developmental condition — on the same terms as any other medical condition.
  But it only reaches plans California regulates. If your employer pays its own claims, federal
  law controls instead, and the state rules don't apply. So the first question isn't "will they
  cover it?" It's "what kind of plan do I have?" This page answers that, then shows you how to
  appeal a no.
</PlainBox>

<div class="cols">
<article class="prose">

The denial letter is written to be read once and believed. It uses the phrase "not medically
necessary" as if that settled something, and it never mentions that an independent doctor
outside your insurance company can be asked to look at the same file — for free, on your
request. Most parents never make that request. Families who do win often enough that the
process exists at all.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="what-must-be-covered">What does California make my health plan cover?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Two rules stack. First, California's <b>parity</b> law: plans issued, amended, or renewed on
  or after January 1, 2021 must cover <b>medically necessary treatment of mental health and
  substance use disorders under the same terms and conditions applied to other medical
  conditions</b> <Cite>HSC §1374.72</Cite>. Second, <b>SB 946</b>: plans that cover hospital,
  medical, or surgical care must also cover <b>behavioral health treatment</b> for autism or
  pervasive developmental disorder <Cite>HSC §1374.73</Cite>. Both apply only to plans
  California regulates — see the next section.
</AnswerFirst>

"Medically necessary" is a coverage term, not a verdict on your child. In this statute it means
care that addresses your child's specific needs, is consistent with generally accepted
standards of care, and is clinically appropriate in type, frequency, extent, site, and duration
<Cite>HSC §1374.72(a)(3)</Cite>. That definition is the ground you stand on in an appeal — and
notice what it turns on: your child's clinician's judgment, not the insurer's budget.

Two consequences of the parity rule that denial letters rarely mention:

- A plan **may not limit** coverage for a mental health condition **to short-term or acute
  treatment** <Cite>HSC §1374.72(a)(6)</Cite>. "We've covered enough months" is not, by itself,
  a lawful reason.
- The care has to sit under the same rules as everything else in the plan. If your child's
  broken arm wouldn't face a visit cap, the same kind of cap on a mental health condition is
  the thing parity exists to stop.

<NoticeBox>
  Coverage is not the same as choosing a therapy. Waypoint doesn't recommend any approach —
  that conversation belongs to you and your child's clinician. Our job is the second question:
  <b>what does your plan have to cover of what the clinician prescribed?</b>
</NoticeBox>

Two more limits worth knowing before you argue with anyone. These mandates don't reduce what
your child is owed by other systems: they do not touch Regional Center or Early Start
entitlements <Cite>HSC §1374.73(a)(3)</Cite>, and they do not reduce anything owed under an
IEP <Cite>HSC §1374.73(a)(4)</Cite>. And they don't switch off the ordinary machinery of
insurance — a plan may still use prior authorization, utilization review, network providers,
copays, and other cost sharing <Cite>HSC §1374.73(g)</Cite>. The mandate says the benefit
exists. It doesn't say nobody will make you fill out forms.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="fully-insured-vs-self-funded">Is my plan fully insured or self-funded?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  This is the question that decides everything else. If your plan is <b>fully insured</b> —
  your employer buys coverage from an insurance company that pays the claims — California's
  mandates apply. If it's <b>self-funded</b> (also called self-insured), your employer pays
  claims from its own money and federal <b>ERISA</b> law controls: state insurance mandates
  don't reach it, because federal law says an employee benefit plan is not <b>"deemed to be an
  insurance company"</b> for purposes of state insurance law <Cite>29 USC §1144(b)(2)(B)</Cite>.
  Big employers are often self-funded, and the card in your wallet may look identical either
  way.
</AnswerFirst>

Here's the mechanism in plain terms. ERISA supersedes state laws that "relate to" an employee
benefit plan <Cite>29 USC §1144(a)</Cite>. States get one carve-out back: ERISA doesn't exempt
anyone from state laws that regulate insurance <Cite>29 USC §1144(b)(2)(A)</Cite>. But then the
"deemer" clause closes the loop — a self-funded employer plan isn't an insurance company, so
laws regulating insurance companies don't land on it <Cite>29 USC §1144(b)(2)(B)</Cite>. A
fully-insured plan is different: the state regulates the insurance company your employer bought
from, and the mandate rides along.

**How to find out for certain, in one phone call.** Ask your employer's HR or benefits contact
this exact question:

*"Is our health plan fully insured or self-funded? I need to know whether California's state
insurance mandates apply to it."*

HR answers this all the time; it is not an unusual or adversarial question. You can also read
it off your plan documents: ask for the **Evidence of Coverage** or **Summary Plan
Description**. A self-funded plan's documents usually name the insurer as an administrator
rather than as the insurer, and often say the plan is governed by ERISA.

Being self-funded is not the end of the road. Many self-funded employers choose to cover
behavioral health treatment anyway — some voluntarily follow the state mandate, and federal
mental health parity rules may still apply to the plan. Ask HR what the plan covers rather than
assuming the answer, and remember that your Regional Center and your school district are
separate doors with their own rules.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="who-regulates">Who regulates my plan — DMHC or CDI?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  California splits this between two agencies. The <b>Department of Managed Health Care
  (DMHC)</b> has charge of the laws governing <b>health care service plans</b> — the category
  California licenses HMOs and many other plans under <Cite>HSC §1341</Cite>. The
  <b>California Department of Insurance (CDI)</b> oversees <b>health insurance policies</b>
  written under the Insurance Code <Cite>INS §10169</Cite>. The rules are near-identical twins;
  what changes is where you send your appeal. Your plan documents name your regulator — that's
  the fastest way to know.
</AnswerFirst>

The mandate is written twice, once in each code, which is why you may see two different
citations for the same right: **HSC §1374.73** for a DMHC-regulated plan and **INS §10144.51**
for a CDI-regulated policy. Both say the same thing about behavioral health treatment. Both run
their own Independent Medical Review system with the same deadlines.

Practically: check the back of your insurance card, then the grievance or complaint section of
your **Evidence of Coverage** — that's where the department handling complaints is named
[TBC: confirm the statutory notice requirement, likely HSC §1368.02, before stating this as a
duty]. If you are still unsure after reading, file with whichever agency you think is right —
they will route it if you're wrong, and being wrong costs you a few days, not your rights.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="how-to-appeal">How do I appeal a denial?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Three steps, and most families stop after one. <b>1. File a grievance</b> (your plan's
  internal appeal). <b>2. After 30 days</b> — or 3 days if it's urgent — you don't have to stay
  in that process any longer <Cite>HSC §1374.30(j)</Cite>. <b>3. Apply for an Independent
  Medical Review (IMR)</b>: doctors outside your insurance company review the file. It costs
  you <b>nothing</b> <Cite>HSC §1374.30(l)</Cite>, and if they say the care is medically
  necessary the plan must authorize it <b>within five working days</b>
  <Cite>HSC §1374.34</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

An IMR is available whenever a service was denied, modified, or delayed **in whole or in part
because it was found not medically necessary** — the statute calls that a "disputed health care
service" <Cite>HSC §1374.30(b)</Cite>. So the phrase in your denial letter that felt like a
closed door is actually the phrase that opens this one.

<DeadlineCallout>
  You have <b>six months</b> from the qualifying event to apply for an Independent Medical
  Review <span class="cite">HSC §1374.30(k)</span> — and you are never required to spend more
  than <b>30 days</b> inside the plan's own grievance process first, or <b>3 days</b> in an
  urgent case <span class="cite">HSC §1374.30(j)</span>. Both agencies' systems work the same
  way <span class="cite">INS §10169</span>.
</DeadlineCallout>

What makes an appeal win is boring and specific: a letter from your child's treating clinician
explaining why this care, at this amount, for this child, meets the medical necessity standard
in the statute; the treatment plan with its measurable goals; and any record of what happened
when the care was reduced or stopped. Write the amount you're asking for and the reason in the
same paragraph. Attach, don't summarize.

And the pressure runs both ways. A plan that drags its feet on implementing an IMR decision
faces an administrative penalty of **at least $10,000 for each day** the decision goes
unimplemented <Cite>HSC §1374.34(b)</Cite>. That is not a number you have to threaten anyone
with. It is a number that explains why, once you win, things usually move.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="how-it-fits">How does insurance fit with the Regional Center and school?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  They are <b>three separate systems</b>, and a no in one says nothing about the other two.
  Your health plan covers what your child's clinician prescribes. Your <b>Regional Center</b>
  funds services under the Lanterman Act. Your <b>school district</b> owes services in the IEP.
  California's insurance mandate explicitly does not reduce Regional Center or Early Start
  entitlements <Cite>HSC §1374.73(a)(3)</Cite>, and does not reduce IEP obligations
  <Cite>HSC §1374.73(a)(4)</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

In practice they interact in one useful way. Regional Centers are expected to use generic
resources — insurance and Medi-Cal — before they purchase a service themselves. But when a
medical service in your child's plan **isn't available within 60 calendar days** through your
insurance or Medi-Cal, the planning team can decide the Regional Center funds it
<Cite>WIC §4646.4(a)(2)</Cite>. That's a sentence worth bringing to your next
<GatedLink href="/guides/regional-centers/ipp-meeting-prep/">IPP meeting</GatedLink>, because a
long insurance waitlist stops being a dead end and becomes a documented fact.

One more boundary: Medi-Cal managed care plans are outside this particular mandate
<Cite>HSC §1374.73(e)</Cite>. Medi-Cal covers children's behavioral health through its own
rules, so if Medi-Cal is your child's coverage, the appeal path is different from the one on
this page.

<EmpathyCallout label="If you're holding a denial letter right now">
  A denial is not a finding about your child. It is a paragraph produced by a process, and the
  same law that let that process happen also built you an independent review with no fee and a
  six-month window. You do not have to feel confident to file it. You only have to send the
  clinician's letter, the treatment plan, and the form. Being tired is not the same as being
  out of options.
</EmpathyCallout>
