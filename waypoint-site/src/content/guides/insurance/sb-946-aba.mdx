<PlainBox>
  SB 946 is California's law saying that if a health plan covers medical care, it also has to
  cover behavioral health treatment for an autistic child. It reaches plans California
  regulates — not employer plans that pay their own claims. The treatment has to be prescribed
  by a doctor or developed by a psychologist, and delivered under a written treatment plan with
  measurable goals. Since January 1, 2026, a plan can't make your child get re-diagnosed just
  to keep the coverage going.
</PlainBox>

<div class="cols">
<article class="prose">

You were told your child needs a certain number of hours a week. Then someone said the words
"not medically necessary," or "we need a new diagnosis first," or "that's the Regional Center's
job." This page is about one narrow, useful thing: exactly what California law requires your
health plan to cover, and how to hold it to that. It is not about which therapy your child
should have. That conversation belongs to you and your child's clinician.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="what-sb-946-requires">What does SB 946 actually require?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Every health plan contract that provides hospital, medical, or surgical coverage must
  <b>also provide coverage for behavioral health treatment for pervasive developmental disorder
  or autism</b> <Cite>HSC §1374.73(a)(1)</Cite>. The coverage must be provided
  <b>"in the same manner and ... subject to the same requirements"</b> as coverage for other
  mental health conditions <Cite>HSC §1374.72</Cite>. Health insurance policies regulated by the
  Department of Insurance carry the identical requirement <Cite>INS §10144.51</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

That phrase — *in the same manner and subject to the same requirements* — is the engine of the
whole thing. It hooks behavioral health treatment onto California's parity law, which says a
plan must cover **medically necessary treatment of mental health conditions under the same
terms and conditions applied to other medical conditions** <Cite>HSC §1374.72(a)(1)</Cite>. So
this is not a small standalone benefit with its own rules. It is a benefit that has to be
treated like the rest of your child's medical care.

Two more duties in the same section, both useful when a plan tells you there's nobody
available:

- The plan **must maintain an adequate network** that includes qualified autism service
  providers <Cite>HSC §1374.73(b)</Cite>. "We have no in-network providers within 60 miles" is
  a problem the plan owns, not one you have to solve.
- The mandate **does not reduce** what other systems owe your child. It doesn't touch Regional
  Center or Early Start eligibility <Cite>HSC §1374.73(a)(3)</Cite>, and it doesn't reduce
  anything owed under an IEP <Cite>HSC §1374.73(a)(4)</Cite>.

And one honest limit: the mandate creates the benefit, not a shortcut past paperwork. Plans may
still use case management, network providers, utilization review, prior authorization,
copayments, and other cost sharing <Cite>HSC §1374.73(g)</Cite>.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="which-plans">Does my plan have to follow it?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  It depends on <b>who pays your claims</b>. If your plan is <b>fully insured</b> — an
  insurance company or health plan pays — California's mandate applies. If your employer is
  <b>self-funded</b> and pays claims from its own money, federal <b>ERISA</b> law controls
  instead: an employee benefit plan is not <b>"deemed to be an insurance company"</b> for
  purposes of state insurance law, so the state mandate doesn't reach it
  <Cite>29 USC §1144(b)(2)(B)</Cite>. Two other exclusions: <b>Medi-Cal managed care</b>
  contracts and specialized plans that don't deliver behavioral health services
  <Cite>HSC §1374.73(e)</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

The card in your wallet looks the same either way, which is why so many families argue the
wrong point for months. Settle it with one email to your employer's HR or benefits contact:

*"Is our health plan fully insured or self-funded? I need to know whether California's state
insurance mandates apply to it."*

If you land in the self-funded column, you have not lost. Many self-funded employers cover
behavioral health treatment anyway, some voluntarily track the state rule, and federal parity
rules may still apply to the plan. Ask HR what the plan actually covers before you assume — and
remember the Regional Center and the school district are separate doors that this answer
doesn't close.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="what-counts">What counts as "behavioral health treatment"?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  The statute defines it as professional services and treatment programs — <b>"including
  applied behavior analysis and evidence-based behavior intervention programs"</b> — that
  develop or restore, to the maximum extent practicable, the functioning of an individual with
  pervasive developmental disorder or autism <Cite>HSC §1374.73(d)(1)</Cite>. Four conditions
  attach: it's <b>prescribed by a physician or developed by a licensed psychologist</b>, it runs
  <b>under a treatment plan</b> from a qualified autism service provider, that plan has
  <b>measurable goals reviewed at least every six months</b>, and it isn't respite, daycare, or
  school services.
</AnswerFirst>

Read that definition once as a checklist, because a denial is very often about one of these four
boxes being unticked rather than about your child at all:

The statute names applied behavior analysis because that is the language the Legislature used.
Waypoint doesn't recommend it, or any other approach. What your child needs is a clinical
question for the people who examine your child; what your plan owes is the legal question this
page answers.

<NoticeBox>
  <b>Before you decide what to ask your plan for, hear the range of views.</b> Autistic adults
  hold a wide range of views about applied behavior analysis — some describe it as helpful,
  and many are critical of it, including of practices that were common in earlier decades.
  Rather than summarize a debate we are not part of, we'd point you to autistic-led
  organizations so you can read it in their own words: the
  <a href="https://autisticadvocacy.org/">Autistic Self Advocacy Network</a> is the most
  commonly cited starting point. Bring what you learn to your child's clinician. That
  conversation is yours; this page only tells you what your plan has to cover of whatever the
  two of you decide.
</NoticeBox>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="medically-necessary">What does "medically necessary" mean here?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  It is a <b>coverage term in the statute</b>, not a rating of your child. Under California's
  parity law it means a service that addresses the patient's specific needs, and is
  <b>consistent with generally accepted standards of care</b> and <b>clinically appropriate in
  type, frequency, extent, site, and duration</b> <Cite>HSC §1374.72(a)(3)</Cite>. A plan also
  <b>may not limit</b> coverage for a mental health condition <b>to short-term or acute
  treatment</b> <Cite>HSC §1374.72(a)(6)</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

Two of those words do the heavy lifting when hours are cut. **Frequency** and **duration** are
part of the medical necessity standard — which means the *amount* of care is a clinical
question governed by the same rule, not a budget line a plan can trim on its own. If your
child's clinician prescribed 20 hours a week and the plan authorized 8, the disagreement is
about medical necessity, and that is precisely the kind of dispute an independent review exists
to settle.

And when a plan says your child has had "enough" months, point at
<Cite>HSC §1374.72(a)(6)</Cite>: coverage for a mental health condition can't be limited to
short-term or acute treatment. Duration is decided by the clinical picture, not by a calendar.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="rediagnosis-2026">What changed on January 1, 2026?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  A plan can no longer make an already-diagnosed child get <b>re-diagnosed to keep coverage
  going</b>. For contracts issued, amended, or renewed on or after <b>January 1, 2026</b>, a
  plan "shall not require an enrollee previously diagnosed with pervasive developmental disorder
  or autism to receive a rediagnosis to maintain coverage for behavioral health treatment"
  <Cite>HSC §1374.73(c)(1)</Cite>. And a plan <b>shall not discontinue or delay existing
  treatment</b> while a rediagnosis is pending <Cite>HSC §1374.73(c)(4)</Cite>. The same rule
  applies to policies at the Department of Insurance <Cite>INS §10144.51(c)</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

This closed a specific trapdoor. Families were being asked to produce a fresh diagnostic
evaluation — often a months-long waitlist — in order to keep services their child was already
receiving. The waitlist became the denial.

Two things the new rule does not do, so you know where the line is. Your child's treating
provider may still **reevaluate** your child to decide what treatment is right, and may still
prescribe a rediagnosis at their own clinical discretion <Cite>HSC §1374.73(c)(2)–(3)</Cite>.
And the plan may still run **utilization review** — the process of approving, changing, or
denying care based on medical necessity — which the statute expressly says is *distinct from* a
rediagnosis <Cite>HSC §1374.73(c)(5)</Cite>.

So if a letter arrives asking for a new diagnostic evaluation as a condition of continuing
coverage, the sentence to write back is short:

*"My child was previously diagnosed and is in active treatment. Please confirm the basis for
requiring a rediagnosis to maintain coverage, and please confirm that treatment will continue
in the meantime."*

<h2 class="qa-h" id="insurance-or-rc">Insurance or the Regional Center — which one pays?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Usually <b>both are in play, in a set order</b>. Regional Centers are expected to use generic
  resources — your insurance and Medi-Cal — before purchasing a service themselves. But if a
  medical service written into your child's plan <b>isn't available within 60 calendar days</b>
  through your insurance or Medi-Cal, the planning team may determine that and authorize
  Regional Center funding <Cite>WIC §4646.4(a)(2)</Cite>. And SB 946 explicitly does not reduce
  Regional Center eligibility <Cite>HSC §1374.73(a)(3)</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

That 60-day rule is the most practical sentence on this page. Waitlists are the real obstacle
for most families, and this turns a waitlist into a documented fact the planning team can act
on. So keep the evidence as it happens: the date you asked, the names of the in-network
providers you called, and what each one said about availability.

Then bring it to the meeting. Ask for the finding in the plan, in writing:

*"We've contacted the in-network providers and the earliest start is past 60 days. Can we
document that the service isn't available within 60 calendar days through our plan, and
authorize it here?"*

has the rest of the scripts and a checklist for that meeting.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="if-denied">What if they deny it anyway?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  File the plan's <b>grievance</b>, then apply for an <b>Independent Medical Review</b> — doctors
  outside your insurance company reading the same file. You cannot be required to stay in the
  plan's grievance process more than <b>30 days</b>, or <b>3 days</b> if it's urgent
  <Cite>HSC §1374.30(j)</Cite>. You have <b>six months</b> to apply
  <Cite>HSC §1374.30(k)</Cite>, and it costs you <b>nothing</b> <Cite>HSC §1374.30(l)</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

<DeadlineCallout>
  Routine authorization decisions are due within <b>5 business days</b> of the plan receiving
  the information it needs, and within <b>72 hours</b> when delay would threaten your child's
  health <span class="cite">HSC §1367.01</span>. A plan that has gone quiet is not simply
  slow — it is past a deadline you can name in writing.
</DeadlineCallout>

Pack the appeal with the four things the statute already asked for: the prescribing clinician's
letter, the current treatment plan with its measurable goals, the most recent six-month review,
and a plain paragraph saying the amount requested and why that amount is clinically appropriate
in frequency and duration <Cite>HSC §1374.72(a)(3)</Cite>. Attach the documents; don't summarize
them.

The full appeal path — grievance, Independent Medical Review, which agency regulates your plan,
and what happens after you win — is in the
<GatedLink href="/guides/insurance/">insurance guide</GatedLink>.

  Reading a statute at the kitchen table after everyone's asleep is not what you pictured for
  this year. But you just learned which law covers your child, which box in a four-part
  definition your denial actually turned on, and that an independent review costs nothing and
  stays open for six months. That's not a small thing to know. Whatever the plan says next, you
  will read it differently now — and that is the whole difference between a letter that ends
