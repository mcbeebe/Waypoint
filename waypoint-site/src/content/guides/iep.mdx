<PlainBox>
  An IEP — Individualized Education Program — is a legal contract between you and your school
  district about what your child gets at school. It starts with a written request for an
  evaluation, and the law puts the clock on the district, not on you: 15 calendar days to send
  you a plan, then 60 days from the day you sign it to test your child and hold the meeting.
  You are a full member of the team that writes the plan. If you disagree with it, you have a
  ladder of options — and the first rung is just asking, in writing.
</PlainBox>

<article class="prose">

Nobody teaches you this system before you need it. You walk into a room with six school staff, a

in it by 8:45am. This page is the whole map — what an IEP is, who qualifies, the sequence and its

<h2 class="qa-h" id="what-is-an-iep">What is an IEP — and how is it different from a 504 plan?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  An <b>IEP</b> is a written, legally binding plan for a child who needs <b>specially designed
  instruction</b> — the school changes <i>what and how</i> it teaches, and owes measurable goals,
  named services, and progress reports <Cite>Ed Code §56345</Cite>. A <b>504 plan</b> is for a
  child whose disability substantially limits a major life activity but who does <b>not</b> need
  special education — it delivers <b>accommodations</b> that change the conditions of learning,
  not the instruction itself <Cite>34 CFR §104.33</Cite>. An IEP carries far more procedure,
  far more paperwork, and far stronger enforcement.
</AnswerFirst>

The plainest way to hold the difference: a 504 plan changes the *road* — extra time, preferential
seating, a quiet room for tests, a health plan for diabetes care. An IEP changes the *car* — a
speech therapist twice a week, a reading program built for dyslexia, a one-to-one aide, goals
written for your child and measured on a schedule.

Section 504 is a civil rights law. It covers any student with a physical or mental impairment
that substantially limits one or more major life activities — the regulation names functions
like caring for oneself, walking, seeing, hearing, speaking, breathing, learning, and working
<Cite>34 CFR §104.3</Cite> — and it requires public schools to provide a free appropriate public
education to those students regardless of the nature or severity of the disability
<Cite>34 CFR §104.33</Cite>. That is a wide net, and it catches many children who are not
IDEA-eligible.

IDEA — the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act — is narrower and richer. The trade is
real: a 504 plan is faster and lighter to obtain; an IEP gives you evaluation rights, a defined
team, prior written notice, and a dispute process with a judge at the end of it. Families
sometimes start with a 504 and move to an IEP as needs become clearer — a normal path, not a
failure of the first plan.

One caution worth saying out loud: "let's just do a 504" is sometimes offered as a shortcut when
a parent has asked for an evaluation. It is not a substitute for one. You can accept a 504 plan
*and* keep your evaluation request running — the two are not a fork in the road.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="who-qualifies">Who qualifies for an IEP in California?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Eligibility is a <b>two-part test</b>, and both halves must be true. First, an evaluation must
  show your child has a disability in one of the categories federal law names. Second, your child
  must — <i>by reason of that disability</i> — <b>need special education and related services</b>
  <Cite>34 CFR §300.8</Cite>. California states the same test in its own words: the IEP team
  identifies the child as a child with a disability whose impairment requires instruction and
  services that <b>cannot be provided with modification of the regular school program</b>
  <Cite>Ed Code §56026</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

The federal categories, as the regulation lists them, are: intellectual disability; hearing
impairment, including deafness; speech or language impairment; visual impairment, including
blindness; emotional disturbance; orthopedic impairment; autism; traumatic brain injury; other
health impairment; specific learning disability; deaf-blindness; and multiple disabilities
<Cite>34 CFR §300.8</Cite>. "Other health impairment" is broader than it sounds and is the
category through which many children with ADHD, epilepsy, or chronic medical conditions qualify.

Now the half that surprises people. A diagnosis is not eligibility. A child can have a clear
medical diagnosis and still not qualify, if the school can meet their needs without specially
designed instruction. And a child can qualify without a tidy diagnosis, because the school's
evaluation — not a doctor's letter — is what decides it. Federal law is explicit that a child who
has one of those conditions but needs **only a related service**, and not special education, is
not eligible under IDEA <Cite>34 CFR §300.8</Cite>. That child may well be a 504 student.

California also lists what may **not** be the primary reason for eligibility: limited English
proficiency, a lack of instruction in reading or mathematics, temporary physical disabilities,
social maladjustment, or environmental and economic factors <Cite>Ed Code §56026</Cite>. This
matters in both directions. It stops a district from labelling an English learner as disabled —
and it also means "he's just behind because of the pandemic" is not, by itself, a lawful basis to
refuse to evaluate a child whose difficulties look disability-related.

If your child is **under 3**, school is not your first door — Early Start through your Regional
Center is, and it runs on a much faster clock. The
<GatedLink href="/guides/regional-centers/">Regional Center guide</GatedLink> has that path, and
the two systems are worth starting in parallel: the school owes your child an education, the
Regional Center funds things school never will.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="the-sequence">How does the IEP process work, start to finish?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Five steps, three of them on legal clocks. You make a <b>written request</b>. The district has
  <b>15 calendar days</b> to send you an assessment plan <Cite>Ed Code §56321</Cite>. You have at
  least <b>15 days</b> to review and sign it <Cite>Ed Code §56321</Cite>. From the day the
  district receives your signed consent, it has <b>60 days</b> to complete the assessments,
  decide eligibility, and hold the IEP meeting <Cite>Ed Code §56344</Cite>. Then services start.
</AnswerFirst>

<DeadlineCallout>
  Assessment plan within <b>15 calendar days</b> of your written request · at least <b>15 days</b>
  for you to decide · <b>60 days</b> from your signed consent to the IEP meeting. School breaks
  longer than 5 schooldays pause both clocks.
  <span class="cite">Ed Code §56321 · §56043 · §56344</span>
</DeadlineCallout>

Three details in that table do most of the work, and they are the ones districts most often
gloss over.

**The clocks are calendar days, with one carve-out.** The 15-day and 60-day windows do not count
days between regular school sessions or terms, or school vacations longer than five schooldays —
and when a vacation ends, the clock picks up again on the day regular schooldays reconvene
<Cite>Ed Code §56321</Cite> <Cite>Ed Code §56344</Cite>. A winter break pauses your clock; a
three-day weekend does not.

**End of the school year has its own rules.** If your referral lands 10 days or fewer before the
regular school year ends, the assessment plan is due within 10 days after the next school year
starts <Cite>Ed Code §56321</Cite>. If the referral comes 30 days or fewer before the year ends,
the IEP is due within 30 days after the next school year starts <Cite>Ed Code §56344</Cite>. This
is why a request sent in early May and a request sent in late May can land months apart.

**The 60 days runs from your signature, not from your request.** Every day an unsigned assessment
plan sits on your kitchen table is a day the evaluation clock has not started. Sign it promptly —
after you have checked the scope.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="what-you-can-insist-on">What can I insist on as a parent?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  More than most parents are told. You can insist that your child be assessed in <b>all areas
  related to the suspected disability</b> <Cite>Ed Code §56320</Cite>; that <b>no single test</b>
  decides eligibility <Cite>Ed Code §56320</Cite>; that the team <b>consider your concerns</b>
  <Cite>Ed Code §56341.1</Cite>; that any refusal come to you as <b>prior written notice</b>
  explaining why <Cite>Ed Code §56500.4</Cite>; and that your child's records be handed over
  <b>within five business days</b> of your request <Cite>Ed Code §56043</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

Here is the working list. None of these are favors.

1. **A full-scope evaluation.** The law requires assessment in all areas related to the suspected
   disability, and names them: health and development, vision, hearing, motor abilities, language
   function, general intelligence, academic performance, communicative status, self-help,
   orientation and mobility, career and vocational abilities and interests, and social and
   emotional status <Cite>Ed Code §56320</Cite>. If a concern you raised is missing from the
   assessment plan, ask in writing for it to be added **before** you sign.
2. **Fair testing.** Assessments must be given in the language and form most likely to yield
   accurate information about what your child knows and can do, must not be racially, culturally,
   or sexually discriminatory, and must be administered by trained personnel
   <Cite>Ed Code §56320</Cite>.
3. **Your concerns on the record.** The IEP team is required to consider your child's strengths,
   the results of the most recent assessment, and *your concerns for enhancing your child's
   education* <Cite>Ed Code §56341.1</Cite>. You have the right to attend, to bring anyone you
   choose, and to present information yourself or through a representative
   <Cite>Ed Code §56341.1</Cite>. Hand your written concerns to the team at the start of the
   meeting and ask that they be attached to the IEP.
4. **Special factors addressed.** If your child's behavior impedes their learning or others', the
   team must consider positive behavioral interventions and supports. It must also consider
   communication needs, braille for a child who is blind or visually impaired, and whether
   assistive technology is required <Cite>Ed Code §56341.1</Cite>.
5. **A real IEP document.** It must contain present levels of academic achievement and functional
   performance; measurable annual goals; how progress will be measured and when it will be
   reported to you; the specific services and supplementary aids, based on peer-reviewed research
   to the extent practicable; an explanation of any time your child will not be with
   non-disabled peers; testing accommodations; and the projected start date, frequency, location,
   and duration of every service <Cite>Ed Code §56345</Cite>. "Speech, as needed" is not a
   service. "30 minutes, twice weekly, in a small group, beginning September 15" is.
6. **A meeting when you ask for one.** You do not have to wait for the annual review. A meeting
   requested in writing to review the IEP must be held within **30 calendar days** of the
   district receiving your request <Cite>Ed Code §56043</Cite>.
7. **The paperwork.** Records within five business days of your request
   <Cite>Ed Code §56043</Cite>, and a copy of your rights and procedural safeguards at least once
   a school year and whenever an initial referral or parent request for assessment happens
   <Cite>Ed Code §56301</Cite>.
8. **An answer in writing.** Whenever the district proposes or refuses to change your child's
   identification, assessment, placement, or education, it owes you prior written notice — a
   document that describes the action, explains *why*, lists every assessment or report it relied
   on, and names the other options the team considered and why they were rejected
   <Cite>Ed Code §56500.4</Cite>. This single document turns "we don't do that here" into
   something you can appeal.

Two more that come up constantly. **Consent is yours to give in pieces:** if you agree with parts
of a proposed program but not all of it, the parts you consent to are implemented, so a
disagreement over one service does not hold the rest hostage <Cite>Ed Code §56346</Cite>. And if
you **disagree with the district's assessment**, you can ask for an independent educational
assessment at public expense — and the district must either pay for it or file for a due process
hearing to prove its own assessment was appropriate <Cite>Ed Code §56329</Cite>. It cannot simply
decline.

<EmpathyCallout label="Before your first IEP meeting">
  You will be outnumbered in that room. That is a fact of the format, not a verdict on your
  standing — you are one member of a team, and the only one who has known this child their whole
  life. Bring your concerns on one page. Bring someone who can take notes while you listen. And
  remember the sentence that resets any meeting that has gone sideways: <i>"Can you put that in
  writing for me?"</i>
</EmpathyCallout>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="after-the-iep">What happens after the IEP is written?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Services begin as soon as possible after the meeting, and the plan gets checked on a schedule:
  the IEP is reviewed <b>at least once a year</b>, and your child is <b>reassessed at least every
  three years</b> — and not more than once a year unless you and the district agree
  <Cite>Ed Code §56043</Cite>. Progress toward each goal must be reported to you on the schedule
  written into the IEP itself <Cite>Ed Code §56345</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

Treat the annual review as the audit it is, not a formality. Before it, read last year's goals and
ask one question of each: did this get measured, and did my child move? A goal with no progress
data is easy to fix, because the law already requires the reporting — you are asking for something
owed, not something new.

The initial IEP also needs your informed consent before special education services begin
<Cite>Ed Code §56346</Cite>. Read it before you sign. The version discussed in the meeting and
the version that arrives in the mail are usually the same; "usually" is why you read it.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="disagree">What if I disagree with the school?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  You have a ladder, and you climb it one rung at a time: <b>ask in writing</b> → <b>request an
  IEP meeting</b> (owed within 30 calendar days <Cite>Ed Code §56043</Cite>) → <b>mediation</b>,
  which is voluntary and is <i>not</i> a prerequisite to anything else
  <Cite>Ed Code §56500.3</Cite> → a <b>compliance complaint</b> → a <b>due process hearing</b>
  before an administrative law judge <Cite>Ed Code §56501</Cite>. Most disagreements end on the
  first or second rung.
</AnswerFirst>

The ladder exists because escalation is expensive — in time, in energy, and in the relationship
with the people who see your child every day. Starting at the top is rarely the fastest route to a
service. But knowing the top rung is there changes how the bottom rungs go. A few anchors to carry
with you:

- **Get the refusal in writing.** A "no" in a meeting starts nothing. Prior written notice does
  <Cite>Ed Code §56500.4</Cite>. Ask for it by name.
- **Mediation is voluntary and lawyer-free.** California's mediation-only conference is not a
  prerequisite to a due process hearing, and attorneys and paid legal advocates may not attend it
  <Cite>Ed Code §56500.3</Cite>. That keeps it a conversation, which is often exactly what is
  needed.
- **Due process is available on the things that matter most:** a proposal or a refusal to change
  your child's identification, assessment, placement, or free appropriate public education
  <Cite>Ed Code §56501</Cite>.
- **Disagree with the testing? Ask for an independent evaluation** at public expense
  <Cite>Ed Code §56329</Cite> — often the single highest-leverage move a parent can make, because
  it changes the evidence rather than arguing about it.

The full ladder, with the deadlines and the exact words for each rung, lives at
<GatedLink href="/guides/iep/dispute-ladder/">Disagreeing with the school: your options in
order</GatedLink>. Free help is available and you never have to pay for it: every California
county is served by a federally funded parent training and information center, and Disability
Rights California takes special education cases.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="start-here">Where should I start today?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  If your child has never been evaluated: send the written request this week — that one email
  starts every clock on this page. If your child already has an IEP and something is wrong: put
  the concern in writing and ask for an IEP meeting, which the district owes you within
  <b>30 calendar days</b> <Cite>Ed Code §56043</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

Districts have a standing duty to find and evaluate children with disabilities who live in their
area — "child find" <Cite>Ed Code §56301</Cite>. You are not asking for an exception. You are
asking them to do a job the law already assigned them, on a schedule the law already set.
