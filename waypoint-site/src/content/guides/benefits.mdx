<PlainBox>
  California has more help for your child than any one office will ever tell you about —
  Regional Center services like respite, Medi-Cal, IHSS, therapies through your insurance,
  and SSI. Each has its own door, its own rules, and its own myths. This page is the map:
  what each resource is, who it's really for, and which door to knock on first. Most of them
  do <b>not</b> depend on how much you earn.
</PlainBox>

<div class="cols">
<article class="prose">

Nobody hands you this list. You find out about respite from another parent in a waiting
room, about deeming from a Facebook group at midnight, about IHSS a year later than you
could have. That drip-feed is the system's biggest failure — so here is the whole map in one
place, with the guides that go deep on each door.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="the-map">The doors, at a glance</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Five systems fund help for a California child with a disability: your <b>Regional
  Center</b> (services and respite, no income test), <b>Medi-Cal</b> (health coverage — with
  a second door that can leave parental income out), <b>IHSS</b> (paid in-home care hours), <b>your health
  insurance</b> (therapies your child's clinician prescribes), and <b>SSI</b> (a monthly
  payment, income-tested). They run independently — being told no at one door says nothing
  about the other four. And alongside them runs a sixth lane, <b>private services</b>, where
  you trade money for speed.
</AnswerFirst>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="regional-center">Regional Center services — including respite</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  If your child is Regional Center–eligible, the center coordinates and funds services —
  <b>respite</b> among them: paid breaks for you, with a trained provider for your child
  <Cite>WIC §4512(b)</Cite>. In our experience it is the support families need soonest and
  hear about last. Services carry <b>no income test and no family fee</b> — California's
  family cost participation and annual family program fees were repealed effective July 1,
  2024 <Cite>DDS</Cite> — but many are <b>not automatic</b>: they attach to needs written
  into your child's IPP, so they have to be asked for <Cite>WIC §4646</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

The pattern to learn early: put every ask in writing, get every answer in writing, and make
sure the need behind each ask lands in the IPP — that's the hook services hang on. Start
with the <GatedLink href="/guides/regional-centers/">Regional Center guide</GatedLink> for eligibility, the
intake clock, and the IPP playbook; if you're not yet in the system, that's door one.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="medi-cal">Medi-Cal — two doors in, one hiding in plain sight</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Door one is income-based, like any family. Door two — <b>institutional deeming</b> — is the
  one nobody tells you about: when a child enrolls in the HCBS waiver for the developmentally
  disabled, the county decides their Medi-Cal with <b>your income left out</b>. It isn't
  automatic and it isn't quick — it runs through your Regional Center, and there are approvals
  on both the Regional Center and the county side — but if you've been told you "make too
  much," you were probably told about door one only.
</AnswerFirst>

Medi-Cal matters even if your child is already insured: it can ride second behind your plan,
and it's the eligibility key for IHSS. The full walkthrough:
<GatedLink href="/guides/benefits/medi-cal-institutional-deeming/">Medi-Cal institutional deeming</GatedLink>.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="ihss">IHSS — the state pays for care hours, including yours</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  In-Home Supportive Services pays for the care and supervision a child needs at home — and
  a <b>parent can be the paid provider</b>. For children who need constant supervision to
  stay safe, <b>protective supervision</b> can carry a family to the program's monthly
  maximum — <b>195 hours</b>, or <b>283</b> if your child is severely impaired.
  The requirement is Medi-Cal — <b>not SSI</b>, whatever the internet told you.
</AnswerFirst>

The path: Medi-Cal first (see above), then the county IHSS application. Everything you need
is in <GatedLink href="/guides/benefits/ihss-protective-supervision/">the IHSS protective supervision guide</GatedLink> — and the myth-buster lives at
<GatedLink href="/answers/does-my-child-need-ssi-to-get-ihss/">Does my child need SSI to get IHSS?</GatedLink>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="therapies">Therapies — your clinician picks, your insurance pays</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Waypoint doesn't recommend therapies — that's a conversation between you and your child's
  doctor or evaluating clinician. Your job is the coverage side: ask your insurer what
  services and therapies your plan covers for your child's diagnosis, and what needs
  pre-authorization. For autism, California law requires most fully-insured plans to cover
  <b>prescribed behavioral health treatment</b> <Cite>SB 946 · HSC §1374.73</Cite>
  (self-funded ERISA plans are exempt) — and every denial you get in writing can be
  appealed, up to an Independent Medical Review.
</AnswerFirst>

Two supplements worth knowing: <b>CCS</b> (California Children's Services) can fund
treatment for specific eligible medical conditions — the income tests are a family adjusted
gross income under {ccsIncomeCeiling()}, or expected out-of-pocket medical costs for the
child above {ccsCostSharePercent()}% of family income (and income isn't a factor at all for
diagnostic services or the school-based Medical Therapy Program). And Regional Center funding
can cover some services insurance won't — another reason the IPP matters.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="private">Private services — the fast lane, if you can fund it</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Alongside the funded systems there's a whole local layer you can simply <b>buy</b>:
  occupational therapy, developmental playgroups (DIR-style and others — whatever your
  child's clinician points you toward), social-skills groups, adaptive sports, and
  specialized or adaptive camps. Insurance <b>may or may not</b> cover them, and Regional
  Center funding <b>requires approvals</b> through the IPP process — but the upside is speed:
  <b>access is almost immediate</b> if you have the resources, while the public clocks run.
</AnswerFirst>

That speed is the honest trade. Every funded door on this page has a waitlist, an
assessment, or an approval attached; the private layer mostly has a phone call and a
calendar. Families who can afford it often use private services as the <b>bridge</b> — start
something now, while the school evaluation, RC intake, and insurance authorizations grind
forward. Three things keep the bridge from becoming a trap:

1. **Keep starting the public clocks anyway.** Paying privately doesn't pause your rights —
   the evaluations and applications above should run in parallel from day one, or you'll
   still be paying out of pocket a year from now for things a system should be funding.
2. **Ask about funding both directions.** Ask your plan whether it covers or reimburses the
   service — and get the answer in writing either way. If your child is Regional
   Center–eligible, ask your service coordinator whether the center can fund it.
   Camps, social recreation, and nonmedical therapies came back into Regional Center
   authority in 2021 <Cite>WIC §4648.5</Cite> — but every purchase runs through an IPP
   approval, so make the ask in writing and early.
3. **Keep every receipt and report.** Private evaluations and progress notes strengthen every
   public case you'll make later — and if a funding door opens, documentation is what any
   reimbursement conversation starts from.

Finding the local layer: the parent networks in the community step of
<a href="/start/">the First 100 Days plan</a> are the best directory that exists —
other families know which local programs, groups, and camps are worth it.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="ssi">SSI — the income-tested one</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  SSI is a monthly federal payment for children with disabilities in lower-income
  households — and in California it brings <b>automatic Medi-Cal</b> with it, no separate
  application needed.
  Parental income is "deemed," but bigger households qualify at higher incomes than most
  families assume — worth a one-minute check on the
  <a href="/tools/ssi-deeming-calculator/">deeming calculator</a> before you rule it out.
</AnswerFirst>

If income rules SSI out, you've lost the smallest door on this page — everything above is
still open. The full picture: <GatedLink href="/guides/benefits/ssi-for-children/">SSI for a child with a disability</GatedLink>.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="order">Which door first?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  For most families: <b>Regional Center intake first</b> (it's the front door to respite AND
  the helper for Medi-Cal deeming), <b>Medi-Cal second</b>, <b>IHSS third</b> — while the
  therapy-coverage conversation with your insurer runs in parallel from day one, and private
  services bridge the waitlists if the budget allows. SSI whenever the calculator says it's
  worth the paperwork. School services run on their own track — see
  <a href="/start/">the First 100 Days plan</a> for the whole sequence.
</AnswerFirst>
