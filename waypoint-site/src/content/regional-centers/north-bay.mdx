<article class="prose">

<AnswerFirst>
  North Bay Regional Center (NBRC) serves <b>Napa, Solano, and Sonoma counties</b> — whole
  counties, no neighbourhood boundaries to work out. It is one of California's <b>21 Regional
  Centers</b> under the Lanterman Act <Cite>WIC §4500 et seq.</Cite>, free to your family at any
  income. Its main office is in <b>Napa</b>. Once intake begins, an assessment is due within
  <b>120 days</b> <Cite>WIC §4643</Cite>; under 3, evaluation and the initial family service plan
  are due within <b>45 days of the referral</b> <Cite>17 CCR §52086</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="who">Is North Bay my Regional Center?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  If your home address is in Napa, Solano, or Sonoma County, yes. Santa Rosa, Petaluma,
  Rohnert Park, Sonoma, Napa, American Canyon, Vallejo, Benicia, Fairfield, Suisun City,
  Vacaville, Dixon, Rio Vista — all NBRC.
</AnswerFirst>

Three counties that do not really behave like one region. Sonoma County looks north and west;
Napa is its own valley; Solano County sits along the I-80 corridor, where plenty of families are
closer to Sacramento or the East Bay than to a Napa office. Your Regional Center is decided by
where you live, though — not by where you work, where your child goes to school, or which
hospital you use.

Two nearby centers get confused with this one all the time. **Marin County is not NBRC** — it
belongs to Golden Gate Regional Center. **Mendocino and Lake counties are not NBRC** either —
they belong to Redwood Coast. If you have moved recently across one of those lines, say so on
the first call: a transfer between centers is a defined process, not a fresh application.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="intake">How does intake actually start here?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Call the main line at the top of this page and ask for intake and assessment. Say your child's
  age in the first sentence, and say which county you live in. The state's directory also lists a
  separate Early Start referral contact for children under 3.
</AnswerFirst>

Expect a real conversation, not a form. Intake here involves an interview about your child's
development, and at some stage a face-to-face meeting with your child. That is normal and it is
how the assessment gets built — but it is also the part that generates travel, so ask early where
it will happen.

Three questions to ask on the first call, and write down the answers with the date:

1. **Which office will hold my child's file, and who is my service coordinator?**
2. **What is the date you received my request?** (Ask for it by email. This is the date every
   clock below runs from.)
3. **Where will the assessment take place, and can any of it happen closer to home?**

<DeadlineCallout>
  <b>Under 3:</b> evaluation, eligibility, and the initial family service plan are due within
  <b>45 days of the referral</b> <Cite>17 CCR §52086</Cite>. <b>Age 3 and older:</b> assessment
  due within <b>120 days of intake</b>, or <b>60 days</b> where a delay would risk your child's
  health, safety, or development <Cite>WIC §4643</Cite>.
</DeadlineCallout>

What to gather: your child's date of birth, your address, any medical or psychological reports,
the most recent IEP or IFSP, preschool or daycare observations, and your own written list of what
an ordinary hard day looks like. A missing report is not a reason to postpone — NBRC funds its
own assessments at no cost to you, and there is no charge for assessment or for service
coordination.

One small piece of housekeeping that pays for itself: photograph every report and letter and keep
the images somewhere you can reach from your phone. Paperwork goes missing, folders get left
behind in a move, and a lost document should never be the reason a clock restarts.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="eligible">Who qualifies?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  A diagnosis is not required to start. At 3 and older, eligibility means a developmental
  disability beginning before age 18 with significant functional limits in <b>3 or more of 7</b>
  life areas <Cite>WIC §4512</Cite>. Under 3, Early Start uses broader delay-or-risk criteria.
  Ages 3 and 4 can also qualify <b>provisionally</b> on <b>2 of 5</b> areas.
</AnswerFirst>

The seven areas are self-care, receptive and expressive language, learning, mobility,
self-direction, capacity for independent living, and economic self-sufficiency — judged against
what is expected at your child's age.

Provisional eligibility is the door most families never hear about: for a 3- or 4-year-old, a
disability that is not solely physical and causes significant limits in at least two of five of
those areas can qualify. It is temporary — the center must reassess before your child turns 5,
and the full three-of-seven standard applies from then <Cite>WIC §4512</Cite>.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="local-vs-statewide">What's the same everywhere, and what's local?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Statewide and non-negotiable: the eligibility standard, the 45- and 120-day clocks, the right to
  a written Notice of Action, and the appeal route. Local: which office you deal with, how far you
  travel, which providers exist in your county, and who your coordinator is.
</AnswerFirst>

That distinction is your best defence against a sentence that starts "well, up here we…". If the
rule is state law, no center can bend it in either direction. If it is local practice, it is a
practice, and practices can be questioned.

Provider supply genuinely does vary across these three counties, and a thin local market is a real
constraint. It is not, however, a lawful reason to leave a needed service out of your child's plan.
If a service is agreed and nobody nearby will deliver it, put that in writing and ask what the
center intends to do about it.

The state's Regional Center directory lists a cultural specialist and a language-access specialist
at every center, NBRC included — a role worth asking for by name if language is making this harder
than it should be.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="appeal">What if North Bay says no?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Ask for it in writing. A denial has to arrive as a <b>Notice of Action</b> setting out the
  decision and your appeal rights. A no delivered on the phone starts no clock and cannot be
  appealed by anyone.
</AnswerFirst>

Say it plainly: *"Please send me that decision as a written Notice of Action."* Then read the dates
printed on the notice and calendar them the day it arrives. Everything after that is California
procedure, identical at all 21 centers.

<GatedLink class="link" href="/guides/regional-centers/appeal-denial/">How to appeal a Regional Center denial, step by step →</GatedLink>

Free help is available and worth using early. The **Office of Clients' Rights Advocacy (OCRA)**,
part of **Disability Rights California**, staffs advocates who handle Lanterman Act appeals for
families at no charge. (Treat them as help, not as the source of the rule — the deadlines that bind
come from the statute and from the dates on your own notice.)

You worked out which of three counties you are in, asked for your date in writing, and learned the
standard before anyone quoted it at you. Most families learn all this the slow way. You did not.
