<AnswerFirst>
  San Andreas Regional Center (SARC) serves <b>Santa Clara, Santa Cruz, San Benito, and
  Monterey counties</b> — one of California's <b>21 regional centers</b> operating under the
  Lanterman Act <Cite>WIC §4500 et seq.</Cite>, free to your family at any income. SARC runs
  offices in <b>San Jose, Watsonville, and Salinas</b>, and it <b>routes intake calls by
  county</b>. For a child <b>3 or older</b>, SARC also accepts an <b>online intake interest
  form</b> — useful if you cannot take a call during business hours.
</AnswerFirst>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="is-this-my-center">Is San Andreas my regional center?</h2>

If you live in **Santa Clara, Santa Cruz, San Benito, or Monterey county**, yes. SARC's boundary
is the county line, not a neighborhood map, so there is nothing subtle to check — unlike Los
Angeles, where seven centers split a single county.

That said, SARC's four counties are four different worlds: Silicon Valley, the Santa Cruz
coast, the Salinas Valley, and rural San Benito. The law is identical in all of them. The travel
time, the office you walk into, and the local provider mix are not.

Counties just outside SARC belong elsewhere — Merced and Madera to
<GatedLink href="/regional-centers/central-valley/">Central Valley Regional Center</GatedLink>,
and the counties north of Santa Clara to
<GatedLink href="/regional-centers/east-bay/">Regional Center of the East Bay</GatedLink> and
its neighbors. What decides it is **where your child lives**, not where they go to school or see
a doctor. Our <a class="link" href="/tools/regional-center-finder/">Regional Center finder</a>
confirms it.

That San Benito wrinkle is worth a sentence, because it is exactly the kind of thing that costs
a family two weeks. If you are in Hollister or anywhere in San Benito county, ask on the first
call: *"Which office will my child's case live in, and who is my intake worker?"* Write the
answer down.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="how-intake-starts">How does intake start here?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Two doors, depending on age. <b>Under 3</b> goes to Early Start, and you call your county's
  intake number to make the referral. <b>3 and older</b> can either call that same county number
  or submit SARC's <b>online intake interest form</b>. Either way, the request is what starts
  the legal clock — so note the date you call or submit.
</AnswerFirst>

The online form matters more than it sounds. Parents working shifts in agriculture, food
service, or care work often cannot make a call between 8 and 5, and "I'll do it tomorrow" turns
into six weeks. A form submitted at 11pm starts the same clock a phone call does.

Before the intake interview, gather what you already have — not a perfect folder:

- Pediatrician notes and any developmental screening results
- Any evaluation: speech, occupational therapy, psychological, audiology
- School or preschool records, an IEP or an IFSP if one exists
- Your own two-paragraph description of a **hard day** — what your child cannot do yet, how
  often, and what it takes out of the household

Missing pieces are not a reason to wait. SARC does its own assessments at no cost to you.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="the-clocks">What are they on the hook for, and by when?</h2>

<DeadlineCallout>
  <b>15 working days</b> from your request to initial intake <Cite>WIC §4642</Cite>.
  <b>120 days</b> from intake to a completed assessment — <b>60 days</b> where delay would risk
  your child's health, safety, or development <Cite>WIC §4643</Cite>. Under 3: <b>45 days</b>
  from referral for the Early Start evaluation and assessment <Cite>17 CCR §52086</Cite>. Then
  <b>60 days</b> from the completed assessment to the written plan <Cite>WIC §4646</Cite>.
</DeadlineCallout>

Those clocks are statewide. They apply to SARC exactly as they apply to every other center, and
they run from **your** request — not from when a worker gets around to opening the file. If a
deadline passes, put it in writing: name the date you first contacted SARC, cite
<Cite>WIC §4642</Cite> or <Cite>WIC §4643</Cite>, and ask for a decision date in reply.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="eligibility">Who qualifies?</h2>

For a child **3 or older**: a developmental disability that began before age 18 and is a
"substantial disability" — significant functional limits in **3 or more of 7 life areas**
(self-care, language, learning, mobility, self-direction, independent living, economic
self-sufficiency) <Cite>WIC §4512</Cite>.

For a child **under 5**, there is a second, lower door most parents never hear about:
**provisional eligibility**, which requires significant functional limits in **at least 2** of
those same areas rather than 3 <Cite>WIC §4512</Cite>. A child found provisionally eligible is
reassessed before turning 5. If your 3- or 4-year-old was told they "just missed" the standard,
ask specifically whether provisional eligibility was considered.

<EmpathyCallout label="From a parent who's been there">
  "I kept waiting until I had a diagnosis, because I thought that was the ticket. It isn't. The
  regional center does its own assessment — my job was just to make the call and then describe
  my kid honestly, on his hardest day, not his best one. The waiting I did on my own was the
  only part nobody was on a clock for."
</EmpathyCallout>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="if-they-say-no">What if San Andreas says no?</h2>

A denial must come as a written **Notice of Action**. A coordinator saying "we don't fund that"
on the phone is not a decision and starts no clock — ask for it in writing. From the written
notice you have **60 days** to request a hearing <Cite>WIC §4710.5</Cite>; check the dates
printed on your own notice.

Free help exists and you should use it. The **Office of Clients' Rights Advocacy (OCRA)**, part
of **Disability Rights California**, has advocates who handle regional center appeals across the
state. Bringing one costs nothing and changes how a meeting goes.

You know which number is yours, what starts the clock, and what a real "no" has to look like in
writing. That is the whole first move.
