<PlainBox>
  Harbor Regional Center is the Regional Center for the south end of Los Angeles County —
  Torrance and the South Bay, the harbor communities, Long Beach, and the Bellflower area. It
  arranges and pays for services for children with developmental disabilities. It is free, at
  any income. You can start with a phone call or with a form on Harbor's own website. Once you
  start, the answer is due in 45 days if your child is under 3, and 120 days if your child is 3
  or older.
</PlainBox>

<div class="cols">
<article class="prose">

<AnswerFirst>
  Harbor Regional Center covers the <b>southern end of Los Angeles County</b> — not the whole
  county. Seven Regional Centers divide LA between them, and the state describes Harbor's share
  as the <b>Bellflower, Harbor, Long Beach, and Torrance health districts</b>. It is one of
  California's <b>21 Regional Centers</b> under the Lanterman Act <Cite>WIC §4500 et seq.</Cite>,
  and services are free to your family at any income. Once you start, the center owes you an
  assessment within <b>120 days</b> of intake <Cite>WIC §4643</Cite> — or, for a child under 3,
  a completed evaluation and initial family service plan within <b>45 days</b> of the referral
  <Cite>17 CCR §52086</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="la-area">Is Harbor my Regional Center?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Your <b>home address</b> decides, not your city name and not your county. Los Angeles County
  is split among seven Regional Centers along <b>health district</b> lines, which are older and
  stranger than city boundaries. Harbor holds the <b>Bellflower, Harbor, Long Beach, and
  Torrance</b> districts.
</AnswerFirst>

"I live in LA County" tells you almost nothing about which center is yours. The county was
carved up decades ago using public health districts, so two houses on opposite sides of one
street can belong to different centers. The state's own directory puts Harbor's share plainly:
"Southern Los Angeles County including Bellflower, Harbor, Long Beach, and Torrance."

In everyday terms, that means the South Bay and the beach cities, the Palos Verdes Peninsula,
the harbor communities on the San Pedro and Wilmington side, the city of Long Beach, and the
southeast cities around Bellflower and Cerritos. It does **not** mean downtown or central Los
Angeles, the Westside, the San Fernando or Antelope Valleys, the San Gabriel Valley, East LA,
or the South Central districts — each of those has its own center. The four health districts
above are what DDS itself publishes; a full city-by-city list isn't, and border cities like
Lomita and Signal Hill aren't confirmed in any official source this page could find — if you're
near a boundary, use the Regional Center finder or call to confirm rather than guess from a
neighborhood name.

Two ways to settle it before you spend a morning on hold. Use the state's lookup at
[dds.ca.gov/rc](https://www.dds.ca.gov/rc/), or start Harbor's own online application — it
asks for your residential ZIP code up front precisely so it can check whether you are inside
the catchment.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="intake">How does intake actually start here?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Two doors, and you can pick either. <b>Call the main line at the top of this page</b> and say
  you want "intake and assessment," or <b>fill in Harbor's online application</b> on its
  website. Whichever you use, the date of that first contact is the date your legal clock
  starts — write it down.
</AnswerFirst>

Harbor is one of the LA-area centers with a genuine web application rather than a phone
number and a hope. The form asks for your child's name and birth date, your residential ZIP,
the languages you speak at home, and a short set of questions about your concerns — a
different question set depending on whether your child is under 3 or 3 and older. You can
attach documents (PDFs and Word files) rather than mailing copies. It also includes an
authorization for the center to do the assessments it needs, which is the same consent you
would sign in person.

If your child is under 3, say so in your very first sentence. Under-3 referrals go to Early
Start, which runs on a different and much faster clock.

<DeadlineCallout>
  <b>Under 3:</b> evaluation, eligibility, and the initial family service plan are all due
  within <b>45 days of the referral</b> <Cite>17 CCR §52086</Cite>. <b>3 and older:</b> the
  assessment is due within <b>120 days of intake</b> — <b>60 days</b> when waiting would risk
  your child's health or safety <Cite>WIC §4643</Cite>. Calendar it the day you first call.
</DeadlineCallout>

Bring, or upload, whatever you already have: pediatrician notes, any evaluation, an IEP or
IFSP, preschool or daycare observations, and your own plain list of what a hard day looks
like. A missing report is not a reason to wait. The center does its own assessments at no cost
to you.

Harbor publishes parent materials in English, Spanish, Korean, Chinese, Tagalog, Vietnamese,
and Khmer — a reflection of who actually lives in Long Beach and the South Bay. Ask for an
interpreter at first contact if you want one; you do not have to bring a bilingual relative to
translate for your own child's case.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="three-doors">Which door does my child's age put us in?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Three, and they have different standards. <b>Under 3</b> is Early Start, which uses broad
  delay-or-risk criteria and needs no diagnosis. <b>Ages 3 and 4</b> can qualify
  <b>provisionally</b> with significant limits in <b>2 of 5</b> life areas. <b>Age 3 and up</b>
  is the full Lanterman standard: <b>3 or more of 7</b> life areas <Cite>WIC §4512</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

The provisional door is the one most families have never heard of, and Harbor names it
directly on its eligibility page. For a young child, California allows eligibility based on a
disability that is not solely physical and causes significant functional limitations in at
least two of five areas — self-care, receptive and expressive language, learning, mobility,
and self-direction. It is not permanent: the center must reassess before your child turns 5,
and the full three-of-seven standard applies then <Cite>WIC §4512</Cite>. Put that date on your
calendar the day provisional eligibility is granted, and use the years between to build the record.

<EmpathyCallout label="A word about the intake interview">
  You will be asked to describe your child, and the honest answer will feel disloyal. Describe
  the hard day. Not the morning they surprised everyone — the afternoon you have not told your
  own mother about. The evaluator is not grading your parenting or your child's worth; they are
  matching a life to a legal standard written in blunt words like "self-care" and
  "self-direction." Saying the hard part out loud is the work. It is not a betrayal.
</EmpathyCallout>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="local-vs-statewide">What's the same everywhere, and what's local?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  The <b>law is identical</b> at all 21 centers — same eligibility standard, same clocks, same
  appeal rights. What changes locally is <b>logistics</b>: which office holds your file, how
  you make first contact, which languages are on hand, and which providers exist near you.
</AnswerFirst>

Do not let anyone tell you a rule is "how Harbor does it" when it is actually state law. The
three-of-seven standard, the 120-day and 45-day clocks, the right to a written Notice of
Action, and the right to appeal are the same in Torrance as they are in Redding.

What is genuinely local: Harbor runs offices in **Torrance** and **Long Beach**, both open
weekday business hours, so ask which one will hold your child's file and who your service
coordinator is. The state's Regional Center directory also lists a **cultural specialist and
language-access manager** at every center, Harbor included — a useful person to ask for if you
are getting nowhere in your own language.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="appeal">What if Harbor says no?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  A "no" only counts if it arrives as a written <b>Notice of Action</b>. An informal no on the
  phone starts no clock and can be appealed by nobody — so ask for the written notice, then
  appeal it. Free advocates who do this every week are available to you.
</AnswerFirst>

The appeal path is identical at all 21 centers, which is good news: you are not learning a
Harbor-specific procedure, you are learning California's.

<GatedLink class="link" href="/guides/regional-centers/appeal-denial/">How to appeal a Regional Center denial, step by step →</GatedLink>

You do not have to do it alone or pay for help. The **Office of Clients' Rights Advocacy
(OCRA)**, part of **Disability Rights California**, provides free advocates who handle
Lanterman Act appeals for families. Ask for them before you write anything. (They are a
resource, not the law — every deadline that matters comes from the statute and from the dates
printed on your own notice.)

You read the standard, you found the right center, and you started the clock on purpose. Most
people never learn how any of this works. You just did.
