  NLACRC is the Regional Center for the north end of Los Angeles County: the San Fernando
  Valley, the Santa Clarita Valley, and the Antelope Valley. It pays for and arranges services

<article class="prose">

<AnswerFirst>
  North Los Angeles County Regional Center (NLACRC) covers <b>three valleys</b> — San Fernando,
  Santa Clarita, and Antelope — which the state also describes as the <b>East Valley, San
  Fernando, and West Valley</b> health districts plus northern LA County beyond the city limits.
  It is one of California's <b>21 Regional Centers</b> under the Lanterman Act
  <Cite>WIC §4500 et seq.</Cite>, free at any income. The clocks: <b>45 days</b> under 3
  <Cite>17 CCR §52086</Cite>, <b>120 days</b> at 3 and older <Cite>WIC §4643</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="la-area">Is NLACRC my Regional Center?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  If you live in the San Fernando Valley, the Santa Clarita Valley, or the Antelope Valley,
  almost certainly yes. Seven centers split Los Angeles County along health-district lines, and
  NLACRC holds the northern share — the largest of the seven by land area, running from the
  Valley floor up past Lancaster and Palmdale.
</AnswerFirst>

In everyday names: Van Nuys, North Hollywood, Sylmar, Pacoima, Chatsworth, Canoga Park,
Northridge, Reseda and the rest of the Valley; Santa Clarita, Newhall and Valencia; Lancaster
and Palmdale in the high desert.

The confusing edge is the eastern one. **Burbank, Glendale, La Cañada Flintridge, La Crescenta
and Pasadena are not NLACRC** — they belong to Frank D. Lanterman Regional Center, even though
they look on a map like they should be Valley-adjacent. The Westside, South Bay, harbor
communities, San Gabriel Valley, East LA and South Central all belong to other centers too.

If you are anywhere near one of those lines, check the state's own lookup at
[dds.ca.gov/rc](https://www.dds.ca.gov/rc/) before you spend a morning on the phone. Reaching
the wrong center does not cost you any rights, but no clock starts until you reach the right one.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="turning-three">My child turns 3 soon. Which application do I use?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  This is the one piece of NLACRC-specific routing every local parent should know. The center's
  own Early Start page says it plainly: <b>if your child is turning 3 within 45 days, use the
  ages-3-and-up Lanterman application instead of the Early Start one.</b>
</AnswerFirst>

The logic is arithmetic, not bureaucracy. Early Start is the program for children from birth to
36 months, and the whole process — evaluation, eligibility, initial family service plan — is due
within 45 days of the referral <Cite>17 CCR §52086</Cite>. If your child ages out mid-process,
you would finish an Early Start determination for a child who is no longer an Early Start
child, then start over.

So count the days to the birthday before you pick a form. If it is more than 45, Early Start.
If it is fewer, go straight to the over-3 track and the three-of-seven standard. If you are
right at the line, call and ask — and ask them to note in your file which track you were told to
use, and when.

The same birthday matters later too. Some children who qualify for Early Start do not meet the
adult-style standard at 3, and that transition catches families off guard. Two-plus years of
early intervention is worth having either way, but do not assume eligibility carries across the
birthday automatically.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="intake">How does intake actually start here?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Call the main line at the top of this page and say you want intake and assessment, or send the
  application to the intake mailbox listed on the center's website. Say your child's age in the
  first sentence — it decides everything about which track you land on.
</AnswerFirst>

For Early Start, NLACRC states the criteria on its own site: a **25% delay in one or more areas**
of development (cognitive, physical, language, social-emotional, adaptive), or a high-risk
profile from multiple risk factors, or an **established risk condition** — a diagnosed medical
condition with a high probability of causing developmental delay. No formal diagnosis is
required to make the referral.

<DeadlineCallout>
  <b>Under 3:</b> evaluation, eligibility, and the initial family service plan are due within
  <b>45 days of the referral</b> <Cite>17 CCR §52086</Cite>. <b>Age 3 and older:</b> assessment
  due within <b>120 days of intake</b>, or <b>60 days</b> where delay would risk your child's
  health, safety, or development <Cite>WIC §4643</Cite>.
</DeadlineCallout>

Have ready: your child's date of birth, your address, any medical or developmental reports, an
IEP or IFSP if one exists, preschool or daycare notes, and your own written list of what a hard
day looks like. Do not wait for a missing report — the center funds its own assessments.

NLACRC publishes applications in **English, Spanish, Russian, Tagalog, Farsi, and Armenian**,
which tells you something true about who lives in these valleys. Ask for an interpreter at first
contact rather than bringing a bilingual relative to translate for your own child's case.

<EmpathyCallout label="The distance nobody warns you about">
  Lancaster to the Valley floor is not a trip you make lightly with a dysregulated toddler in the
  back seat, and the Antelope Valley families know it. Ask, on the first call, which office holds
  your file, whether the assessment can happen closer to home, and whether any of it can be done
  by video. It is a fair question and a common one. You are not being difficult by asking how far
  you will have to drive.
</EmpathyCallout>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="local-vs-statewide">What's the same everywhere, and what's local?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  The law is identical at all 21 centers: the same eligibility standard <Cite>WIC §4512</Cite>,
  the same 45- and 120-day clocks, the same right to a written Notice of Action and an appeal.
  Local means offices, staffing, providers, forms, and routing advice like the turning-3 rule
  above.
</AnswerFirst>

NLACRC organizes itself by valley, with offices serving the San Fernando, Santa Clarita and
Antelope Valley areas. Confirm the address before you drive: official state listings and the
center's own pages have not always agreed on which office is the main one, and this page does
not publish an address list it can't currently verify — get the address from your intake worker
and confirm it before you drive.

The state's Regional Center directory also lists a cultural specialist and language-access
manager at every center, NLACRC included. That is a role to ask for by name if language or
culture is making this harder than it should be.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="appeal">What if NLACRC says no?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  A denial only counts when it is written. Ask for a <b>Notice of Action</b> — the document that
  states the decision and your appeal rights. A no over the phone starts no clock and cannot be
  appealed by anyone.
</AnswerFirst>

The words that work: *"Please send me that decision as a written Notice of Action."* Then read
the dates on it and calendar them the day it arrives. What follows is state procedure, identical
in Palmdale and in Eureka.

<GatedLink class="link" href="/guides/regional-centers/appeal-denial/">How to appeal a Regional Center denial, step by step →</GatedLink>

You do not have to pay for help. The **Office of Clients' Rights Advocacy (OCRA)**, part of
**Disability Rights California**, provides free advocates who handle Lanterman Act appeals for
families. (They are a resource, not the rule — the binding deadlines come from the statute and
from the dates printed on your own notice.)

You counted the days to a birthday, picked the right form, and asked for the decision in
writing. That is exactly the kind of thing that decides these cases, and you did it before
anyone told you to.
