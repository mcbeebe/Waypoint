  Central Valley Regional Center arranges and pays for disability services in six counties in
  the San Joaquin Valley and the Sierra foothills. It is free, whatever you earn, and you do not
  need a diagnosis to start.
  CVRC takes referrals five ways — online, phone, fax, mail, or in person at an office — and
  anyone can make one, including you. Once you ask, the law gives them 15 working days to sit
  down with you.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="who">Who does Central Valley Regional Center serve?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  CVRC serves families living in <b>Fresno, Kings, Madera, Mariposa, Merced, and Tulare
  counties</b> — one of the 21 regional center territories created by the Lanterman Act
  <Cite>WIC §4500 et seq.</Cite> Services are provided regardless of your income, and you do not
  need a completed diagnosis to start. Your home address decides which center is yours.
</AnswerFirst>

That is a big, mostly agricultural stretch of the state — Fresno and Visalia at one end,
Mariposa's mountain communities at the other — and it is one of the most linguistically diverse
territories in California. That matters more here than the map does, and we come back to it
below.

Not sure CVRC is your center? Our
<a href="/tools/regional-center-finder/">Regional Center finder</a> answers it from your county
in a few seconds.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="how-to-refer">How do I make a referral to CVRC?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  More ways than most centers offer. CVRC's own eligibility page says referrals "can be made
  online, by phone, fax or mail," and that it welcomes referrals <b>in person at one of our
  offices</b>. There is also an intake email — <b>IntakeReferrals@cvrc.org</b> — listed both by
  CVRC and by the state's own regional center directory.
</AnswerFirst>

Pick whichever one you will actually finish tonight. A referral by email at 11pm counts exactly
as much as one made by phone at 10am, and it has the advantage of time-stamping itself.

You also do not have to be the one who makes it. For children under 3, CVRC says plainly that
"anyone can make a referral, including parents, medical care providers, neighbors, family
members, foster parents, and day care providers." If your pediatrician offered to send one, let
them — and then send your own anyway, so there is no chance of it sitting in someone else's
inbox.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="offices">Which CVRC office will I deal with?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  CVRC runs <b>three offices — Fresno, Visalia, and Merced</b>. Fresno is the headquarters; all
  three take intake, and all three handle Early Start referrals for children under 3. Call the
  main line at the top of this page if you are not sure which one covers you.
</AnswerFirst>

CVRC lists separate intake numbers for each office. We deliberately publish only the main line
at the top of this page: a main line is always answered and can transfer you, while a direct
intake line can be voicemail — or the wrong one for your child's age. If you would rather dial
your local office directly, take the number from CVRC's own contact page, not from a search
result.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="intake">What happens after I refer — and how fast?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  CVRC schedules an interview with you, gathers records, arranges any evaluations it needs, and
  sends the file to a multidisciplinary team for the eligibility decision. Its own page states
  the interview happens <b>"within 15 working days"</b> — which is not a courtesy, it is the
  deadline California law puts on every regional center <Cite>WIC §4642</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

For children 3 and older, the standard is **substantial disability**: significant functional
limits in **3 or more** major life areas, from a disability that began before age 18
<Cite>WIC §4512</Cite>. Under 3, Early Start is broader — a measured delay, an established risk
condition, or a combination of high-risk factors, with no diagnosis required.

Bring what you have and do not wait for a perfect folder: pediatrician notes, any evaluation or
diagnostic report, an IEP or preschool observation, and your own written list of what a hard day
looks like. When you describe your child, describe the hard days. Assessors are measuring
function, not parenting.

<DeadlineCallout>
  <b>Interview / initial intake: 15 working days</b> from your request <Cite>WIC §4642</Cite>.
  <b>Assessment: 120 days</b> from intake — <b>60 days</b> if delay would put your child at risk
  <Cite>WIC §4643</Cite>. <b>Under 3: 45 days</b> from referral to evaluation and the first
  plan. <b>IPP: 60 days</b> after the assessment is complete <Cite>WIC §4646</Cite>.
</DeadlineCallout>

<h2 class="qa-h" id="language">Can I do all of this in my own language?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Yes — and it is a right, not a favor. California law requires the regional center to
  communicate with you <b>in your preferred language</b> and to give you the plan itself in that
  language <Cite>WIC §4646</Cite>. CVRC publishes its family guides in <b>twelve languages</b>,
  including Hmong, Armenian, Farsi, and both written forms of Chinese.
</AnswerFirst>

The full list on CVRC's eligibility page is English, Spanish, Hmong, Korean, Simplified Chinese,
Traditional Chinese, Tagalog, Vietnamese, Arabic, Armenian, Farsi, and Russian. Ask for an
interpreter when you make the referral, not when you arrive — it is easier to schedule than to
improvise. And do not let a bilingual older child interpret a meeting about their sibling's
disability. That is not their job, and the law does not ask it of them.

<h2 class="qa-h" id="appeal">What if CVRC says no?</h2>

<AnswerFirst>
  Ask for the written <b>Notice of Action</b>. A "no" said on the phone is not a decision and
  starts no clock. From the date you receive the notice you have <b>60 days</b> to request an
  informal meeting, mediation, or a fair hearing <Cite>WIC §4710.5</Cite>. If a service your
  child already gets is being cut and you want it to continue while you appeal, that request
  must go in within <b>30 days</b> of receiving the notice <Cite>WIC §4715</Cite>.
</AnswerFirst>

Write both dates on the notice itself the day it arrives. Then get free help: the **Office of
Clients' Rights Advocacy (OCRA)**, run by **Disability Rights California**, has advocates who
handle Lanterman Act appeals for families at no cost, in multiple languages.
