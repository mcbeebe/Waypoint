### The Chabot Family Navigator
**For families of kids with ADHD, autism, and other learning differences**
*Made by a Chabot parent who's walked this road. You are not the only one —
there are more of us here than you think.*

#### Start where you are

**1 — "Something's going on, but we don't have a diagnosis or a plan."**
- Start with a conversation: ask your child's teacher what they're seeing.
- Ask the office or your teacher to set up an **SST** (Student Success Team
  meeting) — a no-paperwork first step where you and the school compare notes.
- You can also go straight to a free, formal evaluation. Put it in writing —
  one sentence is enough: *"I am requesting a special education assessment for
  my child, [name], under IDEA."* Email it to the principal and keep a copy.
- **The clock:** once your written request is in, the district has **15 days**
  to give you an assessment plan, and after you sign it, **60 days** to finish
  testing and hold the first IEP meeting. (Cal. Ed. Code §§ 56321, 56344 —
  school breaks longer than 5 days pause the clock.)

**2 — "We have a diagnosis, but no school plan."**
- A private ADHD or autism diagnosis does **not** automatically create school
  support — and the school can't require you to get one. There are two kinds
  of plans, and it's okay to ask which fits:
  - **504 Plan** — accommodations (seating, breaks, extra time). Lighter
    weight. Good when your child is doing okay academically but needs support.
  - **IEP** — specialized instruction and services (RSP time, OT, speech,
    a behavior plan). More protection, more process.
- Not sure? Request the IEP evaluation (step 1 above). The team can land on a
  504 if that's the better fit — but the evaluation makes it an informed call.

**3 — "We already have an IEP or 504."**
- You can ask for an IEP meeting **any time**, not just at the annual review —
  in writing, the team has **30 days** to hold it (Ed. Code § 56343.5).
- Before any meeting: ask for the assessment reports and draft goals ahead of
  time, and bring a one-page summary of what you're seeing at home.
- If something isn't working, the first move is always a friendly ask:
  *"Could we look at how [accommodation] is going? Here's what we're noticing
  at home."* Most problems resolve right there.

#### The one-line scripts (copy, paste, send)

| You want | Send this to the principal |
|---|---|
| An evaluation | "I'm requesting a special education assessment for [child] under IDEA. Please send me an assessment plan." |
| An IEP meeting | "I'd like to request an IEP team meeting for [child] to discuss [topic]. What dates work?" |
| A 504 meeting | "I'd like to discuss a 504 plan for [child]. Who is the site 504 coordinator, and when could we meet?" |
| Records | "I'm requesting a complete copy of [child]'s student records, including assessments." |

### Three things veteran parents wish they'd known sooner

1. **Put every request in writing.** A hallway chat starts no clock; an email
   does. Friendly and written beats formal and forgotten.
2. **Start collaborative, always.** Ask first. You can firm up later if an ask
   goes unanswered — and there's a ladder for that — but the relationship with
   this team is a multi-year asset.
3. **You can bring someone.** A friend, another parent from the Group, or a
   DREDF-trained advocate can sit with you in any meeting. You never have to
   do this alone.
