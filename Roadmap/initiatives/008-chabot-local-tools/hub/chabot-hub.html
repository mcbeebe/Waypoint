Community Guidelines

We keep things respectful, helpful, and on-topic.

✅ Please Do

  • Share specific experiences ("My kid tried X and here's what happened")
  • Ask genuine questions
  • Be respectful of different parenting approaches
  • Use first names or initials (protect kid privacy)
  • Flag content that breaks these rules using the flag button

❌ Please Don't

  • Self-promotion: "Try my tutoring service!" (vendors must be pre-approved)
  • Medical advice: "You should try this medication" (doctors decide, not parents)
  • Spam: Repeated posts, off-topic links
  • Personal attacks: Targeting specific people, teachers, or families
  • Sharing others' privacy: Full names, specific school details that identify families

What Happens If Content Gets Flagged?

Posts stay live the moment you share them — there's no waiting for approval. If another parent flags something, a moderator will:

  • Clear the flag if the post is fine — it stays up, nothing changes
  • Hide the post and send a friendly note explaining why, if it breaks a guideline
  • In rare cases: follow up with the poster if they repeatedly violate guidelines

This is a space for trust. We assume good intent and work together.

Chabot Family Navigator

A roadmap for parents: what to ask for, who to ask, and the exact words to use.

🌱 Starting Out (Nothing Named Yet)
▼

Use this path if: Your child hasn't been evaluated yet but you know something's different.

What to do:

  1. Request an SST (Student Study Team meeting) with the school.
  2. Bring observations: "My child struggles with transitions" or "Has trouble following multi-step directions."
  3. Ask: "Is my child eligible for evaluation?"

Timeline: 1–2 weeks to SST; 60 days for evaluation if approved.

💬 Diagnosed But School Looks the Same
▼

Use this path if: Your child has a diagnosis (ADHD, autism, anxiety) but the school says they don't need support.

What to do:

  1. Request a 504 Plan meeting (faster than IEP, same accommodations).
  2. Bring your child's diagnosis and specific examples: "At home, without accommodations, they melt down."
  3. Ask for: small group testing, preferential seating, movement breaks, etc.

Timeline: 1–2 weeks.

🌟 Something Needs Tuning
▼

Use this path if: Your child has an IEP or 504, but it's not working.

What to do:

  1. Request an IEP amendment or 504 revision meeting.
  2. Bring data: "Since we added movement breaks, focus improved 30%."
  3. Ask for specific changes with a deadline: "I'd like this in writing by Friday."

Timeline: 1–2 weeks.

Magic Words (Copy & Paste)

To request an SST:

"I'd like to request an SST meeting. [Child's name] is struggling with [specific example: transitions / following directions / focusing in class]. I'd like to discuss whether an evaluation is appropriate."

To request a 504 Plan:

"[Child's name] has been diagnosed with [ADHD / autism / anxiety]. We'd like to discuss a 504 Plan so [he/she/they] can access accommodations at school."

To ask for a change to an IEP:

"We'd like to request an IEP amendment meeting. Since [specific date], [change you've noticed]. We think [specific accommodation] would help. Can we meet to discuss?"

Key Resources for Families

What's free? What costs? What can insurance cover?

What's Free (Through the School)

If your child has an IEP:

  • Speech therapy, occupational therapy, counseling — covered under IEP services
  • RSP (Resource Specialist) — pull-out support, 1:1 or small groups
  • Classroom accommodations — preferential seating, extra time, sensory breaks
  • FAPE (Free Appropriate Public Education) — your right under federal law (IDEA)

If your child has a 504 Plan:

  • Accommodations only — no direct services, but access to classroom supports
  • Examples: preferential seating, written instructions, movement breaks

What's Covered by Insurance / Medi-Cal

If your family qualifies for Medi-Cal:

  • Physical therapy, occupational therapy, speech therapy
  • Behavioral therapy (ABA-style)
  • Psychiatric appointments and medication management
  • Coverage through Regional Center (California's support system for developmental disabilities)

How to access:

  1. Contact your Regional Center (contact info below)
  2. Request an intake appointment
  3. They'll assess eligibility and connect you with providers

What Costs Out-of-Pocket

  • Private tutoring (unless covered by insurance/scholarship)
  • Private therapy outside insurance/Regional Center network
  • Summer camps and enrichment programs
  • Coaching / life skills support (varies)
  • Educational advocates (if you hire one; many parents do themselves)

How to Ask for What Your Family Needs

Template for school meetings: "Our family would benefit from [specific thing]. Is this something the school can provide under [IEP/504], or would we need to pursue it outside? If outside, are there resources the school recommends?"

What Parents Really Need to Know About IEPs

The essentials: what an IEP is, what's negotiable, and common mistakes to avoid.

What Is an IEP?

An Individualized Education Program is a legal contract between you and the school. It says:

  • Your child has a disability affecting education
  • What services/support the school will provide (speech therapy, OT, RSP, etc.)
  • What goals your child will work toward this year
  • What accommodations (extra time, breaks, etc.) will help

Key thing: The school is legally obligated to follow it. If they don't, you can file a complaint or due process claim.

What's Actually Negotiable (You Have Power Here)

Goals & Objectives
▼

You can push back if goals don't match your child's needs. Example: If the IEP says "improve reading" but your child's real challenge is self-regulation, ask for goals around that instead.

Service Minutes & Frequency
▼

If the school offers 30 min/week of OT but your child needs more, ask for more. You can propose specific changes: "I'd like 60 min/week instead."

Classroom Placement & Accommodations
▼

You can ask for specific teachers, accommodations (preferential seating, written instructions), or a smaller class if that helps your child learn best.

Testing & Assessment Changes
▼

If your child tests better with extra time, in a separate room, or with breaks, these are standard accommodations. Ask for them in writing.

Common Mistakes Parents Make

❌ Signing the IEP on the spot without reading it
▼

Always ask to take it home and read it first. You don't have to sign that day. This is YOUR child's legal plan.

❌ Accepting vague goals like "improve social skills"
▼

Ask for specific, measurable goals. "By June, [child] will initiate one conversation with a peer daily" is better than "improve social skills."

❌ Not requesting a Private Evaluation if you disagree with the school's
▼

You have the right to request an Independent Educational Evaluation (IEE) at the school's expense if you disagree with their assessment. This costs $500–2000 otherwise.

❌ Staying silent in meetings
▼

Bring notes on what you've observed at home. Share specific examples. The school professionals will listen, and it strengthens your child's case.

Before Your IEP Meeting: A Checklist

  • ☐ Write down 3–5 specific behaviors/challenges you've seen at home
  • ☐ Write down what you want the IEP to address this year
  • ☐ Bring any outside evaluations or assessments
  • ☐ Bring a support person (partner, friend, advocate) if you want one
  • ☐ Ask for the IEP draft at least 1–2 days before the meeting
  • ☐ If you disagree on something, it's OK to say "I need to think about this" and reconvene later

Classroom Accommodations & Placement

How to ask, what to expect, and what's reasonable to request.

Standard Accommodations at Chabot

These are common accommodations—feel free to ask for them:

Classroom Setup

  • Preferential seating (close to teacher, away from distractions)
  • Quiet corner for breaks
  • Visual schedule on desk
  • Fidget tool access

Assignments & Testing

  • Extra time on tests (standard: 1.5x time)
  • Separate testing room
  • Testing with breaks allowed
  • Written instructions (not just verbal)

Behavioral Support

  • Movement breaks
  • Noise-canceling headphones
  • Pre-teaching of transitions
  • Clear behavior expectations (posted visually)

Communication

  • Check-in emails from teacher
  • Behavior log home daily
  • Visual behavior cards
  • Home-school communication system (e.g., ClassDojo)

How to Ask for Accommodations

Step 1: Identify the specific challenge

Don't say "My child has ADHD." Instead: "My child has difficulty focusing during independent work when there's background noise."

Step 2: Propose a solution

"I think noise-canceling headphones during independent work time might help."

Step 3: Frame it as a request, not a demand

"Would this be something we could try for a week and see if it helps?"

Step 4: Get it in writing

After the teacher agrees, follow up with an email: "Thanks for agreeing to try the headphones strategy. I'll follow up next Friday to see how it's going."

Red Flags (Things Not to Accept)

  • "We don't have time for that accommodation." If it's in the IEP/504, they legally must do it.
  • "That's not how we do things here." Accommodations are meant to change how things are usually done.
  • "Your child needs to adapt to the classroom, not the other way around." Accommodations are a legal right, not a favor.
  • No progress on an accommodation. After 2–3 weeks with no change, request a new strategy or go back to the drawing board.

Classroom Placement Options

SettingWhat It IsBest For
General Education (Mainstream)Regular classroom, full-time, with accommodationsMost kids. Inclusion is the legal default.
General Ed + RSP Pull-outSpends most time in general class, pulls out for small-group helpKids who need targeted support but can handle mainstream class.
SDC (Special Day Class)Separate class, smaller group, more intensive supportKids with significant support needs or who struggle in mainstream.

Note: Chabot default is inclusion. If the school suggests SDC, ask: "Why? Have we tried accommodations in the mainstream class first?"

How to Ask (Templates & Scripts)

Copy-and-paste sentences that work. The right tone, the right words.

Templates by Situation

Requesting an SST (Student Study Team Meeting)

Email or in-person:

"Hi [teacher/principal],

I'd like to request an SST meeting for [child's name]. I've noticed [specific example: he has trouble transitioning between activities / she gets frustrated with multi-step instructions / they struggle to focus during independent work].

I'd like to discuss whether an evaluation might be helpful. Could we schedule a meeting in the next 1–2 weeks?

Thank you,
[Your name]"

Requesting a 504 Plan

Email to 504 Coordinator:

"Hi [504 Coordinator],

My child, [name], has been diagnosed with [ADHD / anxiety / autism / etc.]. We believe a 504 Plan would help [him/her/them] access the classroom more effectively.

We'd like to request a 504 meeting. Are you available in the next 1–2 weeks?

Thank you,
[Your name]"

Requesting a Specific Accommodation

In-person or email:

"Hi [teacher],

I've noticed that [child] does better when [specific situation: they can wear noise-canceling headphones during independent work / they take a 5-minute break between subjects].

Would it be possible to try this as an accommodation for a week or two and see if it helps? I'm happy to [provide the headphones / work with you on timing].

Thank you,
[Your name]"

Pushing Back if Accommodations Aren't Working

Email or in-person:

"Hi [teacher/IEP team],

We agreed to [accommodation] at the last meeting. I wanted to check in on how it's going. [Child] is still [specific issue]. Do you think we need to try a different approach, or is there something I can do at home to support this?

I'd like to schedule a time to talk about next steps.

Thank you,
[Your name]"

Requesting an IEP Amendment (If Something's Not Working)

Email to IEP coordinator:

"Hi [IEP Coordinator],

We'd like to request an IEP amendment meeting. Since [date], we've noticed [specific change]. We'd like to revise the IEP to [specific change requested].

Could we meet in the next 1–2 weeks?

Thank you,
[Your name]"

Key Phrases That Work

When you want to be heard:

  • "I'd like to share what I've observed..." (instead of "My kid can't...")
  • "Can we try [accommodation] for a week and see if it helps?" (instead of "You HAVE to...")
  • "I appreciate your perspective. Here's what I'm seeing..." (instead of "You're wrong...")
  • "I'd like this documented in writing." (makes it official)
  • "I need to take this home and think about it." (buys you time)

What NOT to Say

  • ❌ "You're not helping my child." (accusatory; they'll get defensive)
  • ❌ "My child has a RIGHT to..." (technically true but comes off aggressive)
  • ❌ "This is illegal if you don't..." (save this for attorneys)
  • ❌ Venting or over-explaining (stick to the facts)
Rule of thumb: Assume good intent. Teachers are usually trying. The system is just hard to navigate. Stay collaborative, stay factual, get it in writing.
