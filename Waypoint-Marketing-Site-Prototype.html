Regional Centers & the Lanterman Act: the complete California parent's guide

MBBy Mike Beebe, parent-founder✓Reviewed by [reviewer seat — recruiting]Updated Sep 2026
Plain-language summary A Regional Center is a state-funded nonprofit that coordinates and pays for services for Californians with developmental disabilities — things like respite, therapies, and case management. Services are free to your family regardless of your income, because they're a right under California's Lanterman Act, not a benefit you have to be poor enough to receive.

What is a Regional Center?

Regional Centers are 21 private nonprofits funded by the State of California to coordinate and fund services for people with developmental disabilities, under the Lanterman Act WIC §4500 et seq. Every Californian lives in exactly one Regional Center's territory, and services are provided regardless of family income.

Think of your Regional Center as two things at once: a front door and a funder. As a front door, it's where your child gets evaluated and, if eligible, assigned a service coordinator — a person whose job is to know your child's case and connect you to services. As a funder, it pays for or arranges services that aren't available through other systems: respite care, behavioral services, day programs, transportation, adaptive equipment, and more.

The Lanterman Act is unusual in American disability law. It doesn't say the state may help your child — it establishes services as an entitlement for people who qualify. That's why there's no income test, no waitlist-by-design, and no "we ran out of funding this year" as a lawful answer. When you hear a parent say "the Regional Center is paying for that," this is the system they're talking about.

Each of the 21 centers covers a specific geographic area. Which one serves you depends only on where you live — not where your child goes to school or gets medical care. If you're not sure which one is yours, our Regional Center finder will tell you in a few seconds.

Is my child eligible?

For children 3 and older, eligibility requires a developmental disability that began before age 18 and is a "substantial disability" — meaning significant functional limitations in at least 3 of 7 major life areas WIC §4512. Qualifying conditions include autism, intellectual disability, cerebral palsy, and epilepsy. Children under 3 go through Early Start, which uses broader criteria — a diagnosis isn't required.

The seven life areas the Regional Center looks at are self-care, receptive and expressive language, learning, mobility, self-direction, capacity for independent living, and economic self-sufficiency. Your child doesn't need to struggle in all seven — significant limits in three or more is the standard. For a young child, evaluators consider what's expected at that age, so "economic self-sufficiency" isn't held against a 4-year-old.

The qualifying conditions are autism, intellectual disability, cerebral palsy, epilepsy, and "other conditions closely related to intellectual disability or requiring similar treatment." A diagnosis alone doesn't guarantee eligibility, and the absence of one of these exact labels doesn't automatically disqualify — the substantial-disability analysis is what matters. That fifth category exists precisely for children whose needs are real but whose diagnosis doesn't fit a neat box.

If your child is under 3, stop reading the fine print and call now. Early Start serves infants and toddlers with developmental delays or risk conditions using intentionally broader criteria than the over-3 standard, and the decision clock is faster — 45 days instead of 120 WIC §4643. Some children who qualify for Early Start won't qualify at age 3, and that's okay: two-plus years of early intervention is worth having either way.

How do I apply — and how long can it take?

Call your Regional Center's intake line and say: "I'd like to request an intake and assessment for my child for Regional Center eligibility." Once intake begins, the center must make an eligibility decision within 120 days — or within 45 days for a child under 3 going through Early Start WIC §4643. You do not need a doctor's referral, a lawyer, or a completed diagnosis to start.

That phone call is the whole secret to starting. Use the words "intake and assessment" — they're the formal trigger. The intake worker will ask about your child's development, your concerns, and your address (to confirm you're in their catchment area). Ask them to confirm, in writing if possible, the date your intake request was received: that's the date the clock starts.

Gather what you have, but don't wait until your folder is perfect: any diagnostic reports or medical records, school or daycare notes, IEP or IFSP documents if they exist, and your own written list of what your child struggles with day to day. The Regional Center will do its own assessments — psychological, developmental, adaptive — at no cost to you. Your job is to paint an honest picture of the hard days, not the best days.

"The intake call sat on my to-do list for six weeks. Making it felt like admitting something I wasn't ready to admit. It was the hardest first step, and it was just a phone call."

What happens at the IPP meeting?

The IPP — Individual Program Plan — is the written plan that drives everything the Regional Center funds for your child WIC §4646. It's built at a meeting between you and your service coordinator, and it's meant to be a collaboration, not a presentation. The key strategy: services flow from goals, so come with your child's goals — and your asks — in writing.

Here's the mechanic most parents learn too late: the Regional Center doesn't fund services in the abstract. It funds services that support written goals in the IPP. If the plan says "Mateo will communicate wants and needs using 3-word phrases," then speech services, a communication device, and parent coaching all have a hook to hang on. No goal, no hook. So before the meeting, write down what you want your child's life to look like in a year — then work backward to the supports that get there.

Bring a one-page list of your asks and hand it over at the start. Being in writing does two things: it keeps the meeting on your agenda, and it creates a record. If a request is declined, ask for the response in writing too — that's what triggers your appeal rights (more on that below). You can bring anyone you want for support: a family member, a friend, an advocate.

The IPP isn't carved in stone. You can request a new IPP meeting when circumstances change — a new diagnosis, a new school situation, a family crisis. You don't have to wait for the annual review to ask for more.

What if they say no?

A denial — of eligibility or of a specific service — must come as a written Notice of Action (NOA) that explains the decision and your appeal rights. Appeal deadlines are short and strict — on the order of 30 days, and sooner if you want services to continue during the appeal (sample day-counts; verify the dates printed on your NOA). A "no" on the phone is not a decision. Ask for the NOA.

That last point deserves repeating, because it's where many families get stuck: an informal no — a coordinator saying "we don't fund that" or "your child probably won't qualify" — has no legal weight and starts no clock. Politely insist: "Please send me that decision as a written Notice of Action." The NOA is what converts a shrug into something you can appeal.

From the NOA, the path runs through an appeal to a fair hearing before an independent judge, usually with an optional informal meeting or mediation first — many disputes settle before a hearing ever happens. Read the deadlines printed on your own NOA carefully and calendar them the day it arrives: filing on time is what preserves your rights, and filing quickly can keep existing services in place while the appeal is decided (sample; confirm the exact windows on your notice).

You don't have to do this alone, and you don't have to pay anyone. The Office of Clients' Rights Advocacy (OCRA) — part of Disability Rights California — provides free advocates who know the Lanterman Act and handle Regional Center appeals for families like yours. Denials get reversed regularly, especially when a parent shows up with the statute, the record, and an advocate. A no today is often a not-yet.

IHSS Protective Supervision: how California families qualify

MBBy Mike Beebe, parent-founder✓Reviewed by [reviewer seat — recruiting]Updated Sep 2026
Plain-language summary IHSS protective supervision can pay a parent for up to 195 hours a month (283 if your child is severely impaired) to keep a child safe who needs constant supervision. You do not need to be low-income, and you do not need SSI. The key that opens the door is Medi-Cal — and a child with a developmental disability living at home can usually get Medi-Cal through institutional deeming, which ignores parental income.

What is protective supervision?

Protective supervision is the part of In-Home Supportive Services (IHSS) that pays for someone — often you, the parent — to observe a child who, because of a developmental disability, would put themselves in danger without it. It is about the safety need, not chores. The rule lives at MPP §30-757.

Think of it as the state recognizing a specific kind of work: the constant, eyes-on vigilance a child needs when they cannot judge danger for themselves. A child who elopes out the front door, climbs to unsafe heights without hesitation, mouths inedible objects, or walks toward a busy street with no sense of risk needs someone watching — not sometimes, but all the time they are awake.

Two things the county is looking for: your child is non-self-directing — meaning that, compared with a typical child the same age, they cannot be trusted to keep themselves safe because of impaired judgment, memory, or awareness — and the need for observation is constant, not occasional. A typical five-year-old also needs supervision; protective supervision covers the supervision your child needs beyond what any child that age would need.

One important boundary: protective supervision is not for housework, meal prep, or medical procedures — IHSS has other service categories for personal care tasks. Protective supervision is the category that recognizes round-the-clock watchfulness as a service in itself.

Does my child need SSI to get IHSS?

No. IHSS requires Medi-Cal, not SSI. Nothing in MPP §30-757 requires an SSI award. A child with a developmental disability who lives at home can qualify for Medi-Cal through institutional deeming — an HCBS waiver rule that ignores parental income entirely.

This is the single most repeated myth online, and it stops families before they start. Here is where it comes from: in California, an SSI award brings automatic Medi-Cal with it. So for many families, SSI happened first and Medi-Cal followed — and the internet quietly rewrote that sequence as a requirement. It never was one.

If your family income is too high for SSI, you have lost nothing. Ask your Regional Center service coordinator about institutional deeming for Medi-Cal, then apply for IHSS. We wrote a short, shareable version of this answer at Does my child need SSI to get IHSS? — and if you are curious whether SSI is on the table anyway, run the SSI deeming calculator.

How do I apply, step by step?

You apply through your county IHSS office, then submit two forms: SOC 821 (the protective supervision assessment) and SOC 873 (a health care certification signed by your child's physician). A county social worker then visits your home to assess need and awards hours — up to 195/month, or 283/month if your child is severely impaired.

Before the home visit, start a supervision log — a simple notebook works. Each time you intervene to keep your child safe, write down the time and what happened. Two weeks of real entries tells the assessor more than an hour of conversation can.

What if the county low-balls the hours?

You can push back, and families regularly win on appeal. Request the county's assessment in writing, then appeal through a state hearing. Your strongest evidence is a supervision log documenting a 24-hour need.

First, ask the county — in writing — for the assessment and hour calculations behind the decision. You are entitled to see how they arrived at the number, and the math often reveals the gap: an assessor who never saw a meltdown, a form that undercounted nighttime waking, a "typical day" that was actually your easiest one.

Second, file for a state hearing. The written notice that came with your hours states the deadline for appealing — calendar it the day the notice arrives. At the hearing you tell your child's story to someone outside the county office, with your documentation in hand.

Third, build the log. Specifics win hearings. For example: a two-week log with an entry for every safety intervention — "6:42am, blocked door, attempted to leave house" (sample); "1:15pm, removed non-food item from mouth" (sample); "3:05am, awake and climbing furniture" (sample). The pattern you live every day becomes evidence the moment you write it down.

Regional Center of the East Bay (RCEB): a parent's field guide

⚠ Waypoint is an independent guide — not affiliated with RCEB, DDS, or any government agency. Contacts verified as of Sep 2026 (sample).
MBBy Mike Beebe, parent-founder✓Reviewed by [reviewer seat — recruiting]Updated Sep 2026
RCEB serves families in Alameda and Contra Costa counties — one of California's 21 regional centers providing services under the Lanterman Act WIC §4500 et seq., regardless of your income. Once intake begins, RCEB owes you an eligibility decision within 120 days — or 45 days if your child is under 3 and coming through Early Start WIC §4643. Write down the date of your first contact. The clock starts then.

How does intake work at RCEB?

You can start by phone or online intake form (sample — see the notice above about contact verification). Either way, the process runs the same. A formal diagnosis helps but is not required to start — eligibility turns on "substantial disability," meaning significant functional limits in 3 or more of 7 life areas WIC §4512.

  1. Contact intake and say your child's age. Under 3 routes to Early Start and the faster 45-day clock; 3 and over goes through standard Lanterman intake.
  2. Gather what you have — pediatrician notes, any evaluations or diagnosis reports, IEP or preschool records, insurance card. Missing pieces should not delay your request.
  3. Do the intake interview. Describe a hard day, not your child's best day. Assessors need the real picture of functional limits.
  4. Note the deadline. The eligibility decision is due within 120 days of intake — 45 under age 3 WIC §4643. Put it on your calendar the day you first call.
  5. Get the decision in writing. If eligible, planning moves to your child's IPP WIC §4646. If denied, you must receive a written Notice of Action (NOA) with your appeal rights.

Request an IEP evaluation: the letter that starts the clock

MBBy Mike Beebe, parent-founder✓Reviewed by [reviewer seat — recruiting]Updated Sep 2026
Once your district receives a written request for a special education evaluation, it has 15 calendar days to respond with an assessment plan — and 60 days after your signed consent to complete the evaluation CA Ed Code §56321. Email the letter below to the principal and the district's special-education director, and keep a dated copy: the clock starts on receipt, not on a phone call.
⏱ Assessment plan due within 15 calendar days of your written request (school breaks over 5 school days pause the clock) · evaluation and IEP meeting within 60 days of your signed consent Ed Code §56321 · §56344

[Date]

Dear [Principal's name] and [Special-education director's name],

I am the parent of [Child's full name] (date of birth [DOB]), a [grade] student at [School name]. After you send it

  1. Calendar day 15. If no assessment plan has arrived by then, follow up in writing and reference your original letter's date — the district's response deadline is 15 calendar days, not business days CA Ed Code §56321.
  2. Sign the assessment plan promptly when it arrives. The 60-day evaluation clock starts at your signed consent, not at your request — the sooner you sign, the sooner it runs.
  3. Check the plan's scope. The evaluation must cover all areas of suspected disability — if a concern you raised isn't listed, ask in writing for it to be added before you sign.

How the deeming math works

Social Security starts with your income, subtracts a living allocation for each non-disabled child, subtracts a parental living allowance, applies the standard $20 general and $65 earned-income exclusions, halves the remaining earned income, and "deems" what's left to your child. If the deemed amount is below the federal benefit rate, your child may qualify for a partial or full benefit. SSA deeming rules

Two things families miss: a child denied SSI for income can still get Medi-Cal through institutional deeming, which ignores parental income entirely — and SSI comes with Medi-Cal automatically in California.
